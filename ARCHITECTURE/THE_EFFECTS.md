# THE EFFECTS — what a ruling changes, taught from thirteen stories

**A tutorial (rewritten 2026-09-07 for the general reader; five
stories from the fourth book added 2026-09-13).** This
document is the companion to THE_LINKS.md. That one shows how the
laws are wired together; this one shows what a law *does* when it
fires — the change it writes down. Thirteen stories are worked in
full — eight from the first three books, five from the fourth —
together covering every kind of change the program can make. The
complete list of changes (1,012 of them as of 2026-09-13 — the number
grows with every stretch of text compiled) is in the program's own
registry file; this document teaches you how to read it.

*Verses are given in plain modern English, rendered from the Hebrew
and checked against it. Where one Hebrew word matters it is shown
once, with its English beside it. Every verse cited is quoted whole.*

---

## The idea in one paragraph

A program that only answers questions is a lookup table: hand it a
case, get a label, it stops. A program whose answers CHANGE something
— open a debt, flip a person's status, start a clock that goes off
years later — is a simulation: it has a world that carries state
forward, and the state today depends on every ruling before it. The
Bible's laws are written the second way. They do not merely classify;
they state consequences, in their own words: he shall pay, he shall
go out free, the land will make good its missed rest years. We call
each such consequence an EFFECT, and this document takes them one at
a time.

Four words you will need, each defined when it first matters below:
an EVENT (something the text says happened), an EFFECT (the change a
ruling makes), the LEDGER (where changes are written), and a TIMER (a
change due on a later day).

## Story 1 — the six-year clock: a servant's term, the ear at the doorpost, and the jubilee

**The story, in plain words.** A man falls into debt, or is sold by
a court for theft, and becomes a Hebrew servant. The law starts a
clock the day he is bought: six years of work, and in the seventh he
walks out free, owing nothing. Some servants do not want to leave —
the master's house is home now, the wife and children are there. The
law lets such a man stay, but only through a public act: his master
brings him to the doorpost and pierces his ear, and the verse says
he now serves "forever." Then the tradition adds the turn that makes
this a story about clocks: forever is not forever. Every fiftieth
year is a jubilee, when every Hebrew servant in the land goes free
at once — and the jubilee frees the pierced man too. So one servant's
life can hold three clocks: the six-year clock that started at the
sale; that clock cancelled at the doorpost; and the jubilee clock
that takes its place.

**What changed, one line each.**
- At the sale: a six-year clock starts on the servant, and "goes
  free" is scheduled for the seventh year.
- At the doorpost: the scheduled "goes free" is cancelled — and in
  its place, "free at the jubilee" is scheduled instead.
- At the jubilee: every Hebrew servant in the land goes free, the
  pierced ones included.

**The verses.** The sale first. Listen for the number and the word
"free." Exodus 21:2:

> "When you buy a Hebrew servant, he works six years; in the seventh
> he goes out free, owing nothing."

The Hebrew for "goes out free" is יֵצֵא לַחָפְשִׁי (yetze la-chofshi,
"he goes out to freedom"). Now the servant who wants to stay. Listen
for "forever." Exodus 21:5-6:

> "But if the servant says outright: I love my master, my wife, and
> my children; I will not go out free — then his master brings him
> before the judges, and brings him to the door or the doorpost, and
> his master pierces his ear with an awl; and he serves him forever."

That last word is לְעֹלָם (le-olam, "forever"). And now the clock
that overrides it, from three books away — the jubilee. Listen for
"freedom" and "everyone." Leviticus 25:10:

> "You shall make the fiftieth year holy, and proclaim freedom
> throughout the land to everyone living in it. It is a jubilee for
> you: each of you returns to his family land, each to his family."

The word for "freedom" here is דְּרוֹר (deror, "liberty").

**What the tradition says.** The tradition's table of decided cases
— the Mishnah, a collection of rulings compiled around the year 200
— lists the ways a servant's term can end, which is exactly a list of
the clocks on his account (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:2):

> "A Hebrew servant is bought with money or with a document, and buys
> himself back by the years, by the jubilee, or by paying off the
> balance… The pierced servant is bought by the piercing, and buys
> himself back by the jubilee or by the master's death."

And the Talmud — the recorded discussions that trace each ruling
back to its verse — says outright that "forever" stops at the jubilee
(Babylonian Talmud Kiddushin 15a):

> "The verse is speaking only about a pierced servant two or three
> years before the jubilee — because the jubilee takes him out."

**The Bible's own record of the law being ignored.** Centuries later
the prophet Jeremiah quotes this law, word for word, to a nation that
had stopped keeping it. Jeremiah 34:14:

> "'At the end of seven years each of you must release his Hebrew
> brother who was sold to you; he serves you six years, then you send
> him out free.' But your fathers did not listen to Me, and did not
> turn their ear."

That is what the later books of the Bible are to this program: not
new laws, but the record of the old ones running — or not running.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** The purchase is an
EVENT: something the text says happened, delivered to the program in
the order the verses stand. The servant law is a RULE that watches
for that event and fires on its own when it arrives. It writes two
lines in the LEDGER — the running set of accounts — on the servant's
account: a six-year term, and "goes free," due six years on. Because
the second is due in the future, it is a TIMER: the program asks its
calendar for the day that is the same date six years later, and
parks the change until the clock reaches it. On "the servant is
pierced" the rule CANCELS that timer and asks the calendar for the
next fiftieth year instead. On "the jubilee is proclaimed" it writes
"goes free" on every account carrying the flag "Hebrew servant." The
two changes it writes — "goes free" and "free at the jubilee" — are
EFFECTS: entries in the fixed list of changes a rule may make, each
with the verse it comes from.

**For the record.** The words "to freedom" stand at exactly THREE
places in the whole Bible, all three in this one chapter. One is the
seventh-year exit above. The other two are the same change fired by a
different event — a master's blow. Exodus 21:26: "When a man strikes
the eye of his male or female servant and destroys it, he must send
him out free in exchange for his eye." Exodus 21:27: "And if he
knocks out the tooth of his male or female servant, he must send him
out free in exchange for his tooth." One effect, three triggers.

## Story 2 — the land's overdue rests: a debt that piles up for centuries, then gets collected

**The story, in plain words.** Every seventh year, the land of Israel
was supposed to rest: no sowing, no pruning, a year off. That was the
law from the start. For hundreds of years the people mostly did not
keep it — they farmed straight through. Each skipped rest year was a
rest the land was still owed. The debt piled up quietly; nobody was
collecting. Then Babylon conquered the country, carried the people
off, and the land lay empty for seventy years. The tradition reads
those seventy years as the collection: the land finally took, all at
once, the seventy rest years it had been owed. When the exile ended,
the Bible's own history book says so in those exact terms.

**What changed, one line each.**
- Every time a seventh year passed unkept, the land was owed one
  more year of rest — a debt on the land's account.
- When the exile came, the people were moved out of the land and
  into their enemies' lands.
- A clock started: the land would lie empty one year for every rest
  it was owed, and stop when the debt was paid.

**The verses.** First the duty. Listen for "the land" as the one who
gets the rest — not the farmer. Leviticus 25:4:

> "But in the seventh year the land gets a complete rest, a sabbath
> for the LORD: you shall not sow your field, and you shall not prune
> your vineyard."

Now the consequence, four verses on in the next chapter. Listen for
the land being described as OWED something and then GETTING it.
Leviticus 26:33:

> "And you I will scatter among the nations, and I will draw the
> sword after you; your land will become a desolation and your cities
> a ruin."

Leviticus 26:34:

> "Then the land will make good its sabbath years — all the days it
> lies desolate while you are in your enemies' land. Then the land
> will rest, and make good its sabbath years."

Leviticus 26:35:

> "All the days it lies desolate it will rest — the rest it did not
> get in your sabbath years, when you were living on it."

A word about that phrase, because the older translations make it
baffling: they say "the land shall be paid HER sabbaths." Who is
"her"? The land. Hebrew makes "land" a feminine word, so old English
carried the "her" across; it means the land itself. And who gets
paid? Also the land. The Hebrew verb is תִּרְצֶה (tirtzeh, "it will
make good," "it will be repaid") — the same verb the chapter uses
nine verses later for the people making good their guilt. The land
is owed rest years; the exile is when it collects them.

The chapter says it again after the people have confessed and
recovered — Leviticus 26:43:

> "The land will be left empty of them and will make good its sabbath
> years while it lies desolate without them; and they will make good
> their guilt — because, yes because, they rejected My rules and
> their soul loathed My laws."

**The tradition's own line.** The oldest running commentary on
Leviticus (the Sifra, a verse-by-verse commentary from the same age
as the Mishnah) puts the whole account in one breath, and its word
for it is OWES (Sifra Bechukotai, chapter 7, row 2):

> "'Then the land will make good its sabbath years' — I told you: sow
> for six years and let the land go for Me in the seventh, so you
> would know the land is Mine. You did not do it. So get up and go
> into exile from it, and the land will take, on its own, every
> release year it owes Me."

And the Mishnah files the punishment under its cause (Mishnah Avot
5:9): "Exile comes into the world for idolatry, for forbidden unions,
for bloodshed — and for not releasing the land."

**The Bible's own record of the debt being collected.** This is the
part a ledger cannot do and a simulation must: a clock set in
Leviticus, going off books later, with the history book quoting the
law at the moment it fires. 2 Chronicles 36:21:

> "to fulfill the word of the LORD spoken by Jeremiah: until the land
> had made good its sabbath years. All the days it lay desolate it
> rested, to complete seventy years."

The prophecy it points to is Jeremiah 25:11:

> "This whole land will become a ruin, a desolation, and these
> nations will serve the king of Babylon seventy years."

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** Three phrases in
Leviticus 26 become three kinds of ledger entry. "Did not rest in
your sabbath years" opens a DEBT on the land's account — one entry
per skipped seventh year. "I will scatter you among the nations" is
a TRANSFER — the people's location changes from the land to the
enemies' lands. "Then the land will make good its sabbath years, all
the days it lies desolate" is a TIMER — a clock that runs one year
per rest owed and stops when the balance is zero, running WHILE the
people are away. The program for this chapter takes two inputs — how
many years the people lived on the land, and how many rest years
they actually kept — computes the debt, and reports how long the
desolation must last. A second function fires the seventy-year clock
from a recorded starting date and reports what stood at the end.
Every effect it writes was found in the chapter's own verbs and
registered before the program ran.

**For the record.** The phrase "the land … make good its sabbath
years" stands at exactly TWO places in the whole Bible: Leviticus
26:34, the law, and 2 Chronicles 36:21, the history quoting the law
as it is fulfilled. And one honest gap: how many rest years were
actually skipped depends on exactly where the seven-year cycle
started, and the program's two ways of placing it give 68 and 69 —
while the Bible's own log says seventy. The tradition has a
reconciliation of that arithmetic (in a chronology called Seder Olam,
not on our shelf), so the program prints the gap instead of hiding
it. Reporting a miss is an older rule in this project than the
effects are.

## Story 3 — the Sabbath: a change that forbids, and a duty that returns every week

**The story, in plain words.** Most of the changes in this program
add something — a debt, a clock, a status. The Sabbath does the
opposite. Every seventh day it forbids: no work, for the whole
household — the father, the children, the servants, the animals,
even the stranger staying in town. Nothing is added to anyone's
account; a door is shut on everyone's. And unlike the servant's
six-year clock, which fires once, this one comes back every week
forever. The program has a name for each half: a BLOCK for the
shut door, and a recurring TIMER for the return.

**What changed, one line each.**
- On the seventh day, work is barred on every member of the
  household — a block, written once on all of them.
- The same day, rest is due — a timer that re-arms itself for the
  next seventh day the moment it fires.
- The block lifts at the day's end; the timer does not.

**The verses.** The commandment. Listen for the list of who is
covered. Exodus 20:10:

> "But the seventh day is a sabbath for the LORD your God: you shall
> not do any work — you, your son, your daughter, your male servant,
> your female servant, your animal, and the stranger within your
> gates."

The same duty stated from the animals' side, in the ordinances.
Listen for "so that." Exodus 23:12:

> "Six days you shall do your work, and on the seventh day you shall
> stop, so that your ox and your donkey may rest, and the son of
> your servant woman and the stranger may catch their breath."

**What the tradition says.** The Mishnah turns "you shall not do any
work" into a list — thirty-nine kinds of labor, with the farm and
the household's crafts in order (Mishnah Shabbat 7:2): "The main
labors are forty less one: sowing, plowing, reaping, binding
sheaves, threshing, winnowing, sorting, grinding, sifting, kneading,
baking; shearing wool, whitening it, combing it, dyeing it, spinning,
weaving…" The program keeps that list as data, not as a rule of its
own; the block is written on the household in the verse's own
words, and the list says what falls under it.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** A BLOCK is a ledger
entry that says an act is barred; it advances no one's state, and
if the barred act appears on the tape while the block stands, the
rule reports it. The Sabbath rule writes one block per member of
the household (the verse names them, so the program names them) —
and because the seventh day returns, the rule's timer carries a
PERIOD: when it fires it writes the block and immediately schedules
itself again seven days on. A recurring duty ends only when the text
says it ends; nothing in the program counts weeks and stops on its
own.

**For the record.** "You shall not do any work" is the first block
in the text that binds animals as well as people; the two verses
above are the only places in the Torah's law that list the animals
by name among those who rest.

## Story 4 — Heaven's ledger: the debt no court collects, and the entry that closes it

**The story, in plain words.** Most debts in the program are owed to
a person and collected by a court. But the Bible names a class of
consequence that no court touches. The clearest words are "that
soul shall be cut off" — a penalty the text assigns to Heaven, not to
judges: no trial, no sentence, no executioner. The program keeps a
second ledger for these, HEAVEN's ledger, and it has entries that
open and entries that close. The entry that closes many of them is
just as explicit in the text: "the priest makes atonement for him,
and he is forgiven." An offering brought, a debt on Heaven's ledger
closed.

**What changed, one line each.**
- Eating leavened bread during Passover opens an entry on Heaven's
  ledger: cut off. No court is named.
- Bringing a sin offering for an unintentional sin closes an entry:
  forgiven.
- The court's ledger is untouched by either.

**The verses.** The debt that only Heaven collects. Listen for who
does the cutting off — no one is named. Exodus 12:15:

> "Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread; on the very first day
> you shall remove leaven from your houses — for whoever eats
> leavened bread from the first day to the seventh day, that soul
> shall be cut off from Israel."

The word is וְנִכְרְתָה (ve-nikhretah, "and she shall be cut off" —
the soul). Now the entry that closes. Listen for the last word.
Leviticus 4:20, the sin offering for the whole community:

> "He shall do with the bull as he did with the bull of the sin
> offering — so he shall do with it; and the priest shall make
> atonement for them, and they shall be forgiven."

The same closing formula on the poor person's bird offering,
Leviticus 5:10: "and the priest shall make atonement for him for the
sin he committed, and he shall be forgiven."

**What the tradition says.** The Mishnah counts the debts of this
class (Mishnah Keritot 1:1): "There are thirty-six cases of cutting
off in the Torah…" — and lists them, each a sin whose penalty the
text assigns to Heaven. The whole tractate is the accounting of
this one ledger.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** HEAVEN is a kind of
ledger entry beside the court's: an obligation that opens on a
person's account with no earthly collector named. Such an entry
stays OPEN until the text records the act that closes it — an
offering brought, a confession made — and the program never closes
it on its own. In the program's output, Heaven's open entries are
printed with the rest at the end of a run; a run may end with some
still open, and that is a legal ending. The closing formula "and he
shall be forgiven" is itself an effect in the registry, named from
the verse, and it is the effect the sin-offering rules write.

**For the record.** The phrase "the priest shall make atonement for
him and he shall be forgiven" closes nine sin-offering cases in
Leviticus 4-5; the machine found it at nine seats, and each is a
closing entry.

## Story 5 — the goring ox: a flag with memory, and a penalty on a body

**The story, in plain words.** An ox gores a person to death. The
law's first ruling is about the ox — it is stoned, and its meat may
not be eaten — and its owner goes free: an animal did it, not him.
But the law then asks a question about the past: was this ox known
to gore? If it had gored before and the owner was warned and did not
guard it, the owner is now guilty too — "the ox shall be stoned, and
its owner too shall be put to death" — with the text at once opening
a door: a ransom may be set, and he pays it and lives. So the ox
carries a FLAG, "forewarned," that changes every later ruling; the
stoning is a penalty written on a body; and the ransom is a debt
that stands in for a life. Three kinds of change in one law.

**What changed, one line each.**
- First goring: a penalty on the ox's body (stoned); nothing on the
  owner.
- After repeated gorings: a flag on the ox — "forewarned."
- The next goring, with the flag set: a penalty on the owner too,
  redeemed by a ransom debt.

**The verses.** The first case. Listen for "the owner is clear."
Exodus 21:28:

> "When an ox gores a man or a woman and they die, the ox must be
> stoned and its meat may not be eaten; and the owner of the ox is
> clear."

The forewarned case. Listen for "from before" and "was warned."
Exodus 21:29-30:

> "But if the ox was a gorer from before, and its owner was warned
> and did not guard it, and it killed a man or a woman — the ox is
> stoned, and its owner too shall be put to death. If a ransom is set
> on him, he pays the redemption of his life, whatever is set on
> him."

The word for "forewarned" is הוּעַד (hu'ad — "it was testified
about"): the owner has been told, before witnesses. And the slave's
case, with a fixed sum, Exodus 21:32: "If the ox gores a male or
female slave, he pays their master thirty shekels of silver, and the
ox is stoned."

**What the tradition says.** The Mishnah defines the flag by a count
(Mishnah Bava Kamma 2:4): "Which ox is innocent and which is
forewarned? Forewarned — any ox about which testimony was given on
three days. Innocent — once it has held back for three days, in the
words of Rabbi Yehuda. Rabbi Meir says: forewarned — any ox about
which testimony was given three times; innocent — once children can
handle it and it does not gore." Two teachers, two ways to set and
clear the flag; the program carries both.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "Forewarned" is a
STATUS on the ox's account — a flag set by the third goring, read by
every later goring. "Stoned" is a BODY entry — a penalty written on
a body, the ox's or, in the second case, the owner's. "Ransom" is a
DEBIT that stands in for the body entry when the court sets it. The
program's own test submits the goring event four times for the same
ox: the first three each write a half payment on the owner (the
innocent ox's rate), the third also flips the flag, and the fourth
writes the full payment because the rule now reads the flag. That
is a machine with memory: the same event, arriving into a different
ledger, gives a different ruling.

**For the record.** "The owner of the ox is clear" and "its owner
too shall be put to death" stand three verses apart; the only thing
between them is the flag.

## Story 6 — the house with the plague: a thing removed from the world

**The story, in plain words.** Leviticus describes a plague that can
appear in the walls of a house. The priest shuts the house for seven
days; if the marks have spread, the affected stones are pulled out
and thrown outside the city, the walls are scraped, and new stones
and plaster go in. Then the house is watched. If the plague comes
back, the whole house is torn down — stones, timber, and all its
plaster — and carried outside the city. And when a house is declared
clean, a strange rite: two birds, one killed, the other dipped in
its blood and sent away over the open field. The program has a kind
of change for the tearing down — DESTROY, a thing removed from the
world — and the bird sent over the field is a TRANSFER out of the
ledger: it leaves and is never accounted for again.

**What changed, one line each.**
- The stones with the plague: pulled out and thrown outside the
  city — destroyed.
- The plague returning after repair: the house demolished —
  destroyed, with everything in it.
- The living bird: sent over the field — transferred out of the
  ledger entirely.

**The verses.** The first repair. Listen for "outside the city."
Leviticus 14:40:

> "The priest shall order that they pull out the stones in which the
> plague is, and throw them outside the city into an unclean place."

The return, and the demolition. Leviticus 14:43 and 14:45:

> "If the plague comes back and breaks out in the house after the
> stones were pulled out and after the house was scraped and
> plastered — he shall tear down the house: its stones, its timber,
> and all the plaster of the house, and carry it outside the city to
> an unclean place."

"He shall tear down" is וְנָתַץ (ve-natatz — "and he shall
demolish"). And the bird, when a house is cleared. Leviticus 14:53:

> "He shall send the living bird outside the city over the open
> field; so he makes atonement for the house, and it is clean."

**What the tradition says.** The Mishnah lays the whole procedure
out as a decision table — ten houses, each a path through the rules
(Mishnah Negaim 13:1): "There are ten houses. One whose plague
faded in the first week or went away — he peels it and it is clean.
One whose plague faded in the second week or went away — he peels it
and it requires the birds. One whose plague spread in the first week
— he pulls out the stones, scrapes, plasters, and gives it a week: if
it came back, he tears it down; if it did not come back, it requires
the birds…" The program's compiled house machine reproduces all ten
paths.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** DESTROY is the kind
of change that removes a thing from the world: the entry is written
on the thing itself — the stones, then the house — and after it the
thing is gone from every later ruling. "Demolished" and "burned in
fire" (the plagued garment's fate) are the registry's own names for
it, taken from the verses' verbs. The bird's departure is a TRANSFER
whose destination is nowhere: the ledger records that it left, and
records nothing after. The house machine is one of the five in the
program that carry state across time (STATE_MACHINES.md in this
folder): the seven-day shut, the repair, the second watch, the
verdict.

**For the record.** The tear-down verb stands at exactly one place
in the whole law of Leviticus, this verse; the Mishnah's ten-house
table was the answer sheet the compiled machine was graded against,
ten of ten.

## Story 7 — the body's own clocks: impure until evening, and counting seven days

**The story, in plain words.** The shortest timer in the whole law
is a status that clears at sunset. Touch the carcass of certain
animals and you are unclean "until the evening" — a few hours, then
it lifts by itself. The longest personal timer is a count a person
keeps: a man recovering from a discharge "shall count for himself
seven days," and only then wash and be clean. Between them they show
the two ways a status can end in this program: by the day's boundary
passing, or by a count the person carries reaching its end.

**What changed, one line each.**
- Touching the carcass: a status on the person, "impure," with an
  end already set — the evening.
- The discharge stopping: a seven-day counter on the person, ending
  in a wash and a clean status.

**The verses.** The evening timer. Listen for the two words at the
end. Leviticus 11:24-25:

> "By these you become unclean: whoever touches their carcass is
> unclean until the evening. And whoever carries any of their carcass
> shall wash his clothes and is unclean until the evening."

The seven-day count. Listen for "count for himself." Leviticus
15:13:

> "And when the man with the discharge is cleansed of his discharge,
> he shall count for himself seven days for his cleansing, and wash
> his clothes and bathe his body in running water, and be clean."

**What the tradition says.** The whole shelf of the Mishnah's
purity tractates — Kelim, Negaim, Zavim, Niddah and the rest — is a
table of these statuses and their clocks; the compiled rules for
Leviticus 12 and 15 were graded against Mishnah Niddah, Zavim, and
Kinnim read whole, 161 rows on the first run. And one thing the
tradition settled that the verse leaves open: the evening that ends
the impurity is the calendar day's boundary, evening to evening, the
same boundary the first chapter of Genesis set with "there was
evening and there was morning."

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "Impure until the
evening" is a STATUS with a TIMER attached at birth: the rule writes
the flag and, in the same breath, schedules its clearing at the next
day boundary. "Counts seven clean days" is a TIMER on the person
whose expiry is a status write — clean. Because the program now
counts days (THE_CLOCK.md), both live in the same world as the
servant's six years; before the clock was rebuilt they could not.
"Impure until the evening" is the most frequent effect in the whole
registry: the machine found its clause at eight verses of Leviticus
11 alone.

**For the record.** The two verses above are the pattern for
forty-two "until the evening" clauses in the Bible, every one a
timer that ends at the same boundary.

## Story 8 — the daily offering: a debt that opens every morning, and the day it stopped

**The story, in plain words.** Every day in the sanctuary, two lambs:
one in the morning, one in the afternoon, "continually." And the
fire on the altar must never go out; every morning the priest adds
wood. These are the program's clearest case of a duty that RECURS —
it comes due every single day — and of a duty that is DISCHARGED by
an act: the lamb is owed at dawn, the lamb is offered, the debt is
closed, and tomorrow's is already scheduled. The tradition even
records the day the daily offering stopped, during the siege of
Jerusalem — the same summer fast day on which the tablets were
broken. So the program has both ends of a recurrence: how it re-arms,
and the text event that ends it.

**What changed, one line each.**
- Each morning: a debt opens on the altar's account — the morning
  lamb owed; each afternoon, the second.
- Each offering brought: the day's debt closed.
- The fire: a standing duty, never to go out, renewed every morning.
- The day the offering ceased: the recurrence cancelled by a
  recorded event, not by a count.

**The verses.** The daily offering. Listen for "continually" and
"day by day." Exodus 29:38-39:

> "This is what you shall offer on the altar: two yearling lambs each
> day, continually. The one lamb you shall offer in the morning, and
> the second lamb you shall offer between the evenings."

Exodus 29:42: "A continual burnt offering throughout your
generations, at the entrance of the tent of meeting before the LORD,
where I will meet you to speak to you there." And the fire.
Leviticus 6:5-6:

> "The fire on the altar shall be kept burning on it; it shall not go
> out. The priest shall burn wood on it every morning, arrange the
> burnt offering on it, and burn the fat of the peace offerings on
> it. A continual fire shall be kept burning on the altar; it shall
> not go out."

**What the tradition says.** The Mishnah records the day the daily
offering ended, in a list of five disasters on one date (Mishnah
Taanit 4:6): "Five things befell our fathers on the seventeenth of
Tammuz, and five on the ninth of Av. On the seventeenth of Tammuz
the tablets were broken, the daily offering ceased, the city was
breached, Apostomos burned the Torah, and an idol was set up in the
sanctuary." The tractate Tamid describes the morning routine — the
lots cast at dawn for who slaughters, who sprinkles the blood, who
clears the ashes, who tends the lamp (Mishnah Tamid 3:1).

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** The daily lamb is a
recurring DEBIT: a timer with a period of one day, which on firing
writes "the daily offering is owed" on the altar's account and
schedules itself for tomorrow. The act "the daily offering is
brought," when the text records it, CLOSES the open entry — the
program's close pairing, which was missing until the clock was
rebuilt and would otherwise have left forty years of paid debts
standing open. The fire is a standing duty of the same shape. And
the recurrence ends only when a text event cancels it — the
offering "taken away" — never because the program counted enough
days. In the program's first run of the three books, the tablets'
forty-day timer fired on exactly the seventeenth of Tammuz the
Mishnah names; the daily offering's ending on that date, centuries
later, waits for the books that narrate it.

**For the record.** "Continually" (תָּמִיד — tamid) is the word the
tradition uses as the daily offering's name; it stands twice in the
three verses above and gives the tractate its title.

## Story 9 — the command and its receipt: a debt the order opens, the doing closes, and a count that becomes a row

**The story, in plain words.** Exodus gives the order for a census —
"when you take the sum" — and does not take it. Numbers takes it, on
its first page. The same happens with the camp's layout, the count of
the Levites, the count of the firstborn, the redemption money: an
earlier book orders, this book does. The text keeps its own receipts
for this: the verse that reports the doing very often ends "as the
LORD commanded Moses." The program reads an order as a debt and the
receipt as its close. The count itself is not a debt but a number —
six hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty — and the
program keeps it the way the text does, as a table, tribe by tribe.
And some of the orders in this book are not done in this book at all:
the cities for the Levites, the six cities of refuge. Their doing is
in Joshua. The debts stand open at the Torah's end, on purpose, each
with the verse that will close it named on the entry.

**What changed, one line each.**
- At the order: a debt on the people's account — "commanded" — whose
  value is the thing ordered.
- At the doing: the debt closed, with the receipt verse named as the
  closer; the count written as a status carrying the number, and as
  rows in the population table.
- At an order whose doing lies in a later book: the debt left open,
  the closing verse named.

**The verses.** The order, three books before. Listen for "when."
Exodus 30:12:

> "When you take the sum of the children of Israel by their numbered
> ones, each shall give a ransom for his soul to the LORD when they
> are numbered, so that there is no plague among them when they are
> numbered."

The doing. Numbers 1:2-3:

> "Take the sum of all the congregation of the children of Israel by
> their families, by their fathers' houses, by the number of names,
> every male by their skulls. From twenty years old and upward,
> everyone who goes out to the army in Israel — you shall number them
> by their hosts, you and Aaron."

And the receipt, sixteen verses on. Numbers 1:19:

> "As the LORD commanded Moses, so he numbered them in the wilderness
> of Sinai."

The formula is כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוָּה יְהוָה אֶת מֹשֶׁה (ka'asher tzivvah
Adonai et Moshe, "as the LORD commanded Moses"). Now an order whose
doing is a book away. Numbers 35:2:

> "Command the children of Israel that they give to the Levites, from
> the inheritance of their possession, cities to dwell in; and
> pasture-land for the cities around them you shall give to the
> Levites."

And its receipt, in the sixth book. Joshua 21:3:

> "And the children of Israel gave to the Levites from their
> inheritance, at the mouth of the LORD, these cities and their
> pasture-lands."

**What the tradition says.** The Talmud asks the ledger's own
subtraction (Babylonian Talmud Bekhorot 5a). The Levite houses, added
one by one, come to 22,300; the verse that totals them writes 22,000.
Where did the three hundred go? Its answer: they were firstborn
themselves, and a firstborn Levite could stand in for no firstborn of
Israel. The program does not correct the verse. It reads the sum,
reads the total, reports the three hundred as the text's own delta,
and carries the Talmud's answer as a data row beside it.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "Commanded" is a DEBIT
written by a specification on its addressee; its value is the thing
commanded. The verse that narrates the doing closes it, and since the
fourth book a close is a line of its own in the program's log, naming
the day and the closing verse. The text's receipt formula is read by a
gate of its own: fifty-eight seats of the short form and eleven of the
long, "according to all that the LORD commanded," and for each the
gate asks the ledger what it closed. "Counted" is a STATUS carrying the
number the reader took off the verse. And the count is also a ROW — a
line in the population table, tribe by tribe and family by family —
which only a rule consuming a verse may write; a hand may not. At the
book's end the table holds 148 rows, and Israel's account carries
fourteen commands, seven of them closed by a verse and seven open to
Joshua's runs.

**For the record.** Of the first census's five orders — the count, the
camp, the Levites, the firstborn, the redemption — all five are closed
inside the book's first four chapters, each by a receipt verse. Of the
book's last law chapter's two, none is: the receipt for the Levites'
forty-eight cities is Joshua 21:41, and the program's reader counts
Joshua's four lots to exactly forty-eight.

## Story 10 — the dedication and the appointed days: a change written into the past, and a duty keyed to the calendar

**The story, in plain words.** Numbers 7 tells the twelve princes'
offerings for the altar's dedication, one prince a day for twelve
days. But the chapter is dated to the day the tabernacle was finished
— a month before the census that opens the book — so by the time the
program reads it, its clock already stands past all twelve days. The
order "one prince per day" is an order whose twelve doings are already
past. The program writes them anyway, each dated to its own day, with
the clock not moved back. Then Numbers 28 and 29 lay out the
offerings calendar: the daily lamb of Story 8, and on top of it an
additional offering on every Sabbath, every new moon, and every
festival day. The daily lamb's duty came back every day; these come
back on days only a calendar can name — "the next Sabbath," "the next
first of the month," "the next Passover."

**What changed, one line each.**
- At "one prince per day": twelve entries, one on each prince, each
  dated to a day already past when the order is read.
- At "the burnt offering of the Sabbath on its Sabbath": a recurring
  debt on the altar's account, keyed to the calendar's Sabbath; seven
  more for the month and the festivals.
- The daily lamb's own standing debt, open since the altar was
  raised: read by the new rule, not written a second time.

**The verses.** The order and its first doing. Listen for the phrase
said twice. Numbers 7:11-12:

> "And the LORD said to Moses: one prince per day, one prince per day,
> they shall bring near their offering for the dedication of the
> altar. And he who brought near his offering on the first day was
> Nahshon son of Amminadab, of the tribe of Judah."

The doubled phrase is נָשִׂיא אֶחָד לַיּוֹם (nasi echad la-yom, "one
prince per day"). Now the calendar's rule. Listen for "on its
Sabbath" and "beside." Numbers 28:10:

> "The burnt offering of the Sabbath on its Sabbath, beside the
> continual burnt offering and its libation."

And the calendar's close, Numbers 29:39:

> "These you shall offer to the LORD at your appointed times, besides
> your vows and your freewill offerings, for your burnt offerings and
> for your meal offerings and for your libations and for your peace
> offerings."

**What the tradition says.** The Sifrei on Numbers (47:1) reads the
order of the twelve days off the text: Moses did not know whether the
princes should bring by their birth order, Reuben first, or by the
order of the march, Judah first, until he was told — by the march.
The program computes the same thing: the twelve days' princes are the
camp's order of chapter 2, not the birth order. And the Talmud (Moed
Katan 9a) reads the twelve days as one continuous run with a Sabbath
inside it. For the additional offerings the tradition has a name of
its own, musaf, "the addition," and a rule about it: the daily and the
additional offerings do not hold each other up (Mishnah Menachot 4:4)
— if one is missing, the other is still brought.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** A RETRO-WRITE is a
ledger write dated behind the clock. The rule that consumes "one
prince per day" asks for the anointing day — the date at 7:1, which
the program had already placed a month before the census — and writes
twelve "dedication offered" statuses on the twelve princes, each due
that day plus its count, every due already past. The program's log
keeps a class for such lines, and the tape carries exactly twelve;
the check is that each due equals the day the text itself stamps on
verses 7:12 to 7:78. "The additional offering owed" is a DEBIT with a
PERIOD, like the daily lamb's — but its period is a calendar word, not
a number of days: the timer asks the calendar for the next Sabbath,
the next new moon, the next first day of Passover, and re-arms itself
on each fire. Eight such timers stand on the altar's account, their
values the tables of bulls, rams, lambs and goats the reader took off
the verses. And the daily lamb's own debt, open on the altar since the
day it was raised, is read by the new rule and not written again: one
act, one writer.

**For the record.** "The burnt offering of the Sabbath on its Sabbath"
stands at one place in the whole Bible. The word "beside," which
stacks the day's offerings on the daily one, stands twelve times
between 28:1 and 29:39, and the program reads the stack off it.

## Story 11 — the docket and the rule installed: the change a case makes to the law itself

**The story, in plain words.** Every change so far was written on an
account — a person, an animal, a house, the land. This one is written
on the law. A man is found gathering wood on the Sabbath. The Sabbath
law is in force and its penalty is death — Exodus said so — but the
text says the *manner* of death had not been declared. So the man is
put in custody and the story stops until the answer comes. The answer
comes as a verse: stoning, by all the congregation, outside the camp.
That verse does two things at once. It sentences this man, and it
installs a rule into a law that already stood, for every case after.
Numbers has four such halts (Leviticus one), and the program was
built to stop at each: a docket that opens, an answer verse that
installs, a deed that closes.

**What changed, one line each.**
- At "they put him in custody": a body entry on the man, open; and a
  declaration owed on the court's docket.
- At the answer verse: a rule installed — a status on an institution,
  its value the law it joins; the docket's entry closed.
- At "and he died": the custody, the death sentence and the stoning
  all closed by the deed.

**The verses.** The halt. Listen for the reason the text gives.
Numbers 15:34:

> "And they put him in custody, because it had not been declared what
> should be done to him."

The words are לֹא פֹרַשׁ (lo forash, "it had not been declared"). The
answer and the deed. Numbers 15:35-36:

> "And the LORD said to Moses: the man shall surely be put to death;
> stone him with stones, all the congregation, outside the camp. And
> all the congregation brought him outside the camp and stoned him
> with stones, and he died, as the LORD commanded Moses."

The other halts say their halt in their own words. Numbers 9:8, the
men unclean at Passover: "And Moses said to them: stand, and I will
hear what the LORD commands concerning you." Numbers 27:5, the
daughters of Zelophehad: "And Moses brought their judgment near
before the LORD."

**What the tradition says.** The Sifrei on Numbers (114) reads the
verse exactly as the program does — the penalty already stood, the
mode did not, and the answer is two things:

> "But is it not written (Shemot 31:14) 'He who profanes it shall be
> put to death'? What, then, is the intent of 'For it was not made
> clear'? He did not know with what specific type of death until it
> was told to Him by the Holy One. 'And the L-rd said to Moses: Die,
> shall die the man': this is the judgment for all the generations.
> 'stone him with stones': in this particular instance."

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "In custody" is a BODY
entry — the registry's own row from the prison in Genesis 40, reused
here — written on the man and open. The declaration owed sits on the
court's DOCKET, the program's list of cases no rule decided; the
running world's docket holds four, each with the verse that closed
it. "The rule installed" is a STATUS written on an institution — the
tent of meeting, or the priesthood — whose value is the law it
installs, named in the case's name; the running world holds seven
such rows, and the tradition's own dispute about what the answer is,
a rule for the generations or an edict for this instance, is a
setting with the other arm named. The deed closes the rest. Here is
the man's whole account as the program prints it today, five rows, the
other columns cut:

```
  labor_barred                 | block  | written_by=law_sabbath   | verse=Num 15:32-33 | state=written
  put_to_death                 | body   | written_by=law_sabbath   | closed_by=Num 15:36 | state=closed
  stoned                       | body   | written_by=law_sabbath   | closed_by=Num 15:36 | state=closed
  warned_specifying_the_labor  | status | written_by=law_mekoshesh | verse=Num 15:32-33 | state=written
  in_custody                   | body   | written_by=law_tent      | closed_by=Num 15:36 | state=closed
```

What to notice in the third column: the block and the death sentence
were written by the Sabbath rule installed in Exodus — the first time
that rule ever fired on the tape — and the custody by the tent's own
rule. A law from one book, a case from another, one account.

**For the record.** The phrase "a statute of judgment" — the text's
own name for a rule for the generations — stands at exactly two
places in the Torah: Numbers 27:11, the daughters' answer, and
Numbers 35:29, the refuge cities. The program found both.

## Story 12 — the manslayer's term: a change that ends at a death nobody can date

**The story, in plain words.** A man kills without meaning to. The
avenger of blood may pursue him, so the law gives him six cities to
run to, and the court delivers him from the avenger's hand and sends
him back to his city — to live there "until the death of the high
priest." Then he goes home. Compare the servant of Story 1: his term
had a length, six years, and the program could set a clock. The
manslayer's term has an end but no length. Nobody knows when the high
priest will die. So the program cannot set a timer. It writes an open
entry that carries the name of the priest in office, and closes it
when the text records that priest's death — and only that priest's.

**What changed, one line each.**
- At the court's deliverance: "dwells in refuge" on the manslayer, a
  body entry, open, its value the name of the high priest in office.
- No timer set — the text gives no day.
- At "the high priest died": the entry closed, by the name it carried;
  "returns to the land of his possession" written on him.

**The verses.** The cities. Listen for "unintentionally." Numbers
35:11:

> "And you shall appoint for yourselves cities, cities of refuge they
> shall be for you; and a manslayer who strikes a soul unintentionally
> shall flee there."

The term. Listen for the end that is named and the day that is not.
Numbers 35:25:

> "And the congregation shall deliver the manslayer from the hand of
> the avenger of blood, and the congregation shall return him to his
> city of refuge, where he had fled; and he shall dwell in it until
> the death of the high priest, who was anointed with the holy oil."

Those last words are עַד מוֹת הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדֹל (ad mot ha-kohen
ha-gadol, "until the death of the high priest"). And the release,
Numbers 35:28:

> "For in his city of refuge he shall dwell until the death of the
> high priest; and after the death of the high priest the manslayer
> shall return to the land of his possession."

**What the tradition says.** The Mishnah's table for this law (Mishnah
Makkot 2:6) is a table of which death ends which term. Three kinds of
high priest end it — the one anointed with oil, the one who wore the
many garments, the one who served and was relieved. If the high priest
died after the verdict, the man goes home; if he died before the
verdict was given and another was appointed, the man goes home at the
second priest's death. And the mothers of the high priests, it
records, used to feed and clothe the exiles, so that they would not
pray for their sons to die. The Talmud (Babylonian Talmud Makkot 11a)
counts the words: "the death of the high priest" is written three
times in these verses, one for each of the three.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "Dwells in refuge" is a
BODY entry — the same kind as custody in Story 11 — written open, with
a VALUE: the name of the office-holder, which the refuge rule gets by
a call into the law of chapter 20, where Eleazar took the office at
Aaron's death. "The high priest died" is an EVENT, and when it arrives
the rule closes every open "dwells in refuge" whose value is that
priest's name — a close BY VALUE — and writes "returns to the land of
his possession," a STATUS, on each. Nothing on the tape sends anyone
to a city in the Torah's own story, so the running world holds no
such entry; the program's test world runs the tradition's cases, and
there a man sentenced under Eleazar goes home at Eleazar's death while
a man sentenced under Phinehas stays through it and goes home at
Phinehas's — the Mishnah's row, and the reason the entry is keyed by a
name rather than by the office. Eleazar's death is Joshua 24:33,
outside the Torah: the closer by design, like the debts of Story 9.

**For the record.** "He has no blood" — the words that free the
avenger who kills the manslayer outside his city (35:27) — is the
burglar's clause from Exodus 22:1, in the singular here and the plural
there; the program reuses the burglar's own effect rather than writing
a second. One effect, two laws, as "goes free" was one effect with
three triggers in Story 1.

## Story 13 — the vow: a debt toward Heaven, and the day her father hears it

**The story, in plain words.** A man vows, and is bound by his own
word: "he shall not profane his word." Nobody collects this debt but
Heaven — it is Story 4's kind of entry, opened by the vower himself. A
daughter in her father's house, or a wife, is bound the same way, with
one difference: her father or her husband may confirm the vow or annul
it, and only on the day he hears of it. If he is silent through that
day, the vow stands. If he restrains her that day, the vow falls "and
the LORD will forgive her." And if he annuls it after having let it
stand, the annulment is void, she is clear — and "he shall bear her
iniquity." The chapter has no numbers at all except its clocks: "on
the day he hears," "from day to day," "after he has heard."

**What changed, one line each.**
- At the vow: a debt on the vower toward Heaven, its value the vow.
- At the hearing: a clock to the day's end. Silence through it
  confirms — a status on the vow. Restraint before it cancels the
  clock, closes the debt, and writes Heaven's "annulled": the LORD
  forgives her.
- At an annulment after the confirmation: nothing on her; "her
  iniquity borne," Heaven's entry, on the husband.

**The verses.** The man's vow. Listen for "profane" and "all."
Numbers 30:3:

> "A man, when he vows a vow to the LORD, or swears an oath to bind a
> bond upon his soul, shall not profane his word; according to all
> that goes out of his mouth he shall do."

The bond is לֹא יַחֵל דְּבָרוֹ (lo yachel devaro, "he shall not profane
his word"). The daughter, and the day. Listen for "on the day he
hears" and the last clause. Numbers 30:6:

> "But if her father restrains her on the day he hears, none of her
> vows or her bonds that she bound upon her soul shall stand; and the
> LORD will forgive her, because her father restrained her."

The day is בְּיוֹם שָׁמְעוֹ (be-yom shom'o, "on the day he hears"). And
the late annulment, Numbers 30:16:

> "But if he annuls them after he has heard, then he shall bear her
> iniquity."

**What the tradition says.** The Sifrei on Numbers (156:1) reads the
clock two ways off the chapter's own words and keeps both. "From day
to day" — twenty-four hours? No: "he has confirmed them, for he was
silent on the day of his hearing" — until nightfall. And Rabbi Shimon
ben Yochai: twenty-four hours, for "the Torah said from day to day."
On the late annulment the Sifrei (156:2) reads "after he has heard" as
after he has confirmed, and "he shall bear her iniquity" as: he enters
in her place for the sin.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "The vow bound" is a
DEBIT toward HEAVEN — a debt with no earthly collector, like the cut
off of Story 4, but opened by the vower's own mouth; its value is the
vow. The hearing day is a TIMER, the engine's own, due at the end of
the day of hearing; its fire writes "the vow confirmed," a STATUS.
Restraint before the fire cancels the timer, closes the debt by
value, and writes "the vow annulled" — HEAVEN's entry, whose verse
ends with the same closing formula as the sin offering's, "and the
LORD will forgive her." Annulment after the fire writes nothing on
her and "her iniquity borne," HEAVEN's entry, on the husband. A father
over a married woman, or a husband over a widow, writes nothing at
all: the wrong authority. The two settings of the clock, nightfall and
twenty-four hours, are a calendar row with two arms, the first
running and the second kept; and two questions the Talmud leaves
standing are carried as rows marked unresolved. The program's test
ran the tradition's cases through this machine: eight timers set,
five fired, three cancelled, every number predicted by script before
the run and matched on the first.

**For the record.** "And the LORD will forgive her" stands at three
places in the whole Bible, all three in this chapter (30:6, 30:9,
30:13). "On the day of his hearing" stands four times in the chapter,
"from day to day" once, "after his hearing" once — the chapter's only
numbers are its clocks, and the program found no other.

## How to read the registry

The effects — 1,012 as of 2026-09-13, 140 of them citing a verse of
Numbers — live in one file (World/step9/effect_vocabulary.yaml)
and every entry has the same shape. Here is the one from Story 1:

- **its name:** goes free
- **in plain English:** the servant's status ends — a person's flag
  flips from servant to free
- **the Hebrew it comes from:** יֵצֵא לַחָפְשִׁי ("he goes out to
  freedom"), with the three verses where the machine found it
- **what kind of change it is:** a STATUS — a flag on an account.
  The registry has eight kinds, counted as of 2026-09-13: STATUS 650;
  HEAVEN 99 (an entry on Heaven's account for wrongs no court
  collects); DEBIT 64 (a debt opens); TRANSFER 59 (a location moves);
  TIMER 50 (a change due later); BLOCK 40 (an act barred); BODY 39 (a
  penalty on the body); DESTROY 11 (a thing removed from the world).
- **where it was seen working:** the compiled law that emits it, and
  the test in which it was graded.

Three rules govern the list. An effect enters only when a verse is
found stating it — never because a programmer thought it would be
useful. A rule that tries to write a change not on the list is
refused at the door. And a rule writes only the ledger, never the
tape: it may say a debt is owed, it may never say a thing happened.
Obligations are computed; acts come from the text.

Three lines in the program's own log sit beside the effects without
being effects, and the fourth book brought each of them into use: a
RETRO-WRITE, a write dated behind the clock (Story 10); a ROW, a line
in the population table (Story 9); and a CLOSE, which since 2026-09-12
is a line of its own, so the ledger can say on which day an entry was
closed and by which verse (Stories 9, 11, 12 and 13).
