# The Day We Ran the Law

*A conversation between two friends — neither of them a programmer —
about what happened when a machine read the law code of Exodus and
produced the rabbis' rulebook. Everything they describe really ran:
nine sections of law, forty-four checks, forty-four matches.
Written to be listened to.*

## Part One — "It's written in cases"

**JACK:** Okay, you've been talking about this all week. Start from
the beginning, and remember I fix furnaces for a living.

**MIRIAM:** That's actually perfect, because the whole thing starts
with something you'd recognize — a service manual. You know how a
manual says: *if the pilot light is out, do this; if it's lit but
the burner won't fire, do that?*

**JACK:** Sure. Condition, then instruction.

**MIRIAM:** The law section of Exodus is written that way. Really
written that way, in the Hebrew. There are two little words doing
the work. The word כִּי — *ki*, meaning "when" — opens a new
situation. And the word אִם — *im*, meaning "if" — opens the
variations inside it. "WHEN you buy a Hebrew servant — six years he
serves, in the seventh he goes free. IF he came alone, he leaves
alone. IF he was married, his wife leaves with him. IF his master
gave him a wife..." One "when," then a ladder of "ifs."

**JACK:** Huh. Like an outline. When — and then the sub-cases
underneath.

**MIRIAM:** Exactly like an outline. And here's the first thing that
made my jaw drop. The team had a computer read those two chapters of
Exodus looking for nothing but those two little words. No commentary
loaded, no tradition, nothing — just "find every sentence that
starts with WHEN, and every IF underneath it." The computer chopped
the text into paragraphs all by itself. And then they opened the
Talmud — there's a page, in the tractate called Bava Metzia, where
the ancient rabbis say: the verses about people watching other
people's property divide into *three passages* — this one, this one,
and this one.

**JACK:** And?

**MIRIAM:** The computer's three paragraphs and the rabbis' three
passages were the same three. To the verse. A machine found the
same structure in an afternoon that the rabbis described eighteen
hundred years ago.

**JACK:** Okay, that's a little spooky.

**MIRIAM:** It gets better. You'd think a word like "when" is just a
word. But the Talmud actually keeps a dictionary entry for it.
There's a line — tractate Gittin, page 90 — where a sage named
Reish Lakish says: the word *ki* has four distinct meanings — if,
perhaps, rather, because. That's not a sermon. That's a usage note.
The old teachers knew the little word was doing four different jobs,
and they wrote down the four jobs so you could read the law
correctly.

## Part Two — the property-keepers, and the hidden question

**JACK:** All right, give me a real example. Walk me through one law
like I'm holding the wrench.

**MIRIAM:** The property-keepers. Exodus, chapter 22, nine verses.
Three "when" paragraphs in a row, and they're all the same
situation: I hand you something of mine to watch, and something
goes wrong. Paragraph one: I gave you money or dishes to keep, and
they were stolen. If the thief is caught, he pays double. If he's
not caught — you, the keeper, come to court and swear you didn't
touch it, and you're off the hook.

**JACK:** Seems fair.

**MIRIAM:** Paragraph two: I gave you my donkey or my ox to keep,
and it died, or got hurt, or was carried off, and nobody saw. You
swear, and you're off the hook — same as before. *But* — and this
is the strange part — if it was *stolen* from you, you pay me.

**JACK:** Wait. In the first paragraph, stolen means you swear and
you're free. In the second, stolen means you pay?

**MIRIAM:** You just did, in ten seconds, exactly what the Talmud
does. Because here's the thing the text never tells you: what's
*different* about the two keepers? The verses just say "a man gives
his fellow something to keep" — both times. Same words.

**JACK:** So why different rules?

**MIRIAM:** The Talmud's answer — and I want you to hear how
mechanical this is — is: compare the outcomes. Feed both paragraphs
the same event, theft, and look at what comes out. The first keeper
swears and walks. The second keeper pays. So the second keeper must
be carrying more responsibility. And who carries more
responsibility? Someone who's *being paid* for the job. So paragraph
two is the paid guard, and paragraph one is the unpaid favor for a
neighbor. The Torah never says the words "paid" or "unpaid" there —
the rabbis worked out the hidden difference from the way the
outcomes differ.

**JACK:** Like figuring out which thermostat wire is which by
flipping the breaker and seeing what turns off.

**MIRIAM:** That is *precisely* it. And the third paragraph says
"when a man *borrows*" — that one labels itself — and the borrower
pays for everything, even accidents. The Talmud explains why: all
the benefit is his. He got the use for free, so he carries all the
risk.

**JACK:** Okay. And the machine?

**MIRIAM:** The machine did the whole thing. It read the nine
verses raw, cut them into the three paragraphs by the "when" word,
pulled out each branch — stolen, died, torn by animals, owner
present — and what happens in each. Then it applied the rabbis'
recorded reasoning, step by step, each step labeled with the page
of Talmud it comes from. And at the end it printed the finished
table: who swears, who pays, in every situation. Then they graded
that table against the Mishnah — which is the ancient rulebook that
states the answers — and it matched. Twelve boxes out of twelve.

**JACK:** The Mishnah being...?

**MIRIAM:** Think of it as the answer key. Around the year 200, the
rabbis wrote down thousands of decided cases — situation in,
verdict out — organized by topic. It almost never explains itself.
It just states the answers. Which used to bother people. Now it
looks like exactly what you want sitting next to a machine: the
sheet you grade the machine against.

## Part Three — forty-four for forty-four

**JACK:** So that was one law. You said nine.

**MIRIAM:** The owner of the project said "run it," so they ran the
whole law code — Exodus 21 through 23. Nine sections of law,
forty-four separate checks. I'll give you the tour at walking
speed.

The *Hebrew servant*: the text has a timer — six years, free in
the seventh 
— and the rulebook lists three ways out: serve the years, buy
yourself out, or the Jubilee. The years are right there in the
verse. The buy-out the rabbis derive from a redemption clause a few
verses down. And the Jubilee — that's a nationwide release year
declared in a whole different book, Leviticus — reaches in and
frees him too. A rule from one book overriding another book. The
Talmud shows the wiring.

The *four kinds of damage*: the rulebook opens its damages volume
with "there are four primary categories — the ox, the pit, the
grazing animal, the fire." Guess what the machine found when it
listed the "when" paragraphs in that stretch of Exodus? Four cases
in a row: an ox gores, a pit is left open, an animal grazes a
neighbor's field, a fire spreads. The rulebook's table of contents
is the text's own case list, in order.

**JACK:** And they say the four behave differently?

**MIRIAM:** The Mishnah compares them like an engineer comparing
machines — the ox moves and means to, the pit just sits there, the
fire moves but the wind drives it — and then states what they all
share: the owner was responsible for guarding it, and when it does
damage, he pays from the best of his land. And "best" isn't the
rabbis being poetic — the word is in the verse: "the best of his
field and the best of his vineyard shall he pay."

The *goring ox* deserves its own minute, because it's a machine
with a memory. First offense, the ox is called תָּם — *tam*,
"innocent" — and the owner pays half the damage, and only up to the
value of the ox itself. The verse actually says: sell the live ox
and split the money. But if the ox has a record — the verse says
"a goring ox from yesterday and the day before, and its owner has
not secured it" — now it's מוּעָד, *forewarned*, and the owner pays
in full. And how many offenses flip it? The rabbis counted the
verse's own time-words: "yesterday" — one. "The day before" — two.
"And he has not secured it" — now, the third. Three gorings and the
status changes. Two sages, Abaye and Rava, each counted those words
slightly differently — and the Talmud kept *both* counts, with
their names on them.

**JACK:** They kept the disagreement?

**MIRIAM:** They keep every disagreement. In this project that's not
a bug — when the rabbis disagreed, the machine reports both answers
with the names attached, because the disagreement *is* the record.

And two quick ones. The *injury payments*: the rulebook says one who
injures another pays five things — the damage, the pain, the
doctor, the lost wages, the humiliation. Two of those five are
sitting in plain sight in the verse: "he shall pay for his idleness,
and he shall surely heal" — lost wages and medical bills, right
there in Exodus 21:19. And the *thief's multiples*: steal and it's
found on you, pay double; slaughter or sell a stolen ox, pay five;
a stolen sheep, pay four. The rulebook says the four-and-five rule
applies *only* to ox and sheep — and that's not interpretation,
that's the verse: those constants are attached to exactly those two
animals and nothing else.

**JACK:** So when you add it all up —

**MIRIAM:** Forty-four checks, forty-four matches. And here's the
number I can't stop thinking about: they tagged every single box
with where it came from. About *sixty percent* came straight out of
the bare text — no commentary needed, a machine can point at the
words. The rest splits into two piles: boxes filled by a recorded
rabbinic argument — and I mean recorded, page and name — and boxes
that are additions the tradition itself openly routes in, like that
Jubilee rule reaching over from Leviticus. Sixty percent ink. Nobody
guessed it was that high until somebody counted.

## Part Four — three finds from the same afternoon

**JACK:** You said they found new things while putting this
together. Give me one.

**MIRIAM:** I'll give you three, they're short. First: the
*seducer's fine.* Exodus says a man who seduces a young woman pays
"money according to the dowry of the virgins." Notice what's
missing: the amount. The verse holds a *label*, not a number. Where
does the number live? A different book — Deuteronomy, chapter 22 —
where the same kind of case says: fifty pieces of silver. The
tradition connects the label in Exodus to the number in
Deuteronomy. One book names the price; the other book holds the
price list.

**JACK:** Like a part number in the manual and the price in the
catalog.

**MIRIAM:** Stop being better at this than me. Second: the *eye and
the tooth.* If a master destroys his slave's eye, the slave goes
free. Knocks out his tooth — free. And the Talmud asks the obvious
question, almost in your words: fine, eye and tooth are *written* —
what about an ear? A finger? And the answer they derive: eye and
tooth are examples of a category — parts of the body that don't
grow back. And then the rulebook, in a completely different volume,
enumerates that category: twenty-four tips of the limbs. Two
examples in the verse, one principle behind them, twenty-four
members in the list.

Third, and this one is sobering: the *injured pregnant woman.* Men
are fighting, a pregnant woman is struck, she miscarries. The verse
says the one responsible pays — "by the judges." Pays *what*,
though? How do you put a number on that? The rulebook states the
court's method: appraise what she would be worth before, and what
after, and pay the difference. An actual valuation procedure — with
a named rabbi's objection to it, preserved right there in the text.

**JACK:** Even the objection.

**MIRIAM:** Always the objection. That's the whole character of this
thing. The answer, the reasoning, the dissent, the name.

## Part Five — the book that owes a book

**MIRIAM:** Now the discovery that reordered the whole plan.
There's a famous line in that law code — "an eye for an eye." And
for as long as anyone's been reading, the rabbis have said: it
means *money* — compensation, the value of the eye, not the eye.
Fine. But when the team traced the Talmud's actual proof of that,
the verses the argument runs on aren't in Exodus at all. They're in
*Leviticus, chapter 24* — where the phrase appears for the second
and only other time in the whole Bible, and where the text adds:
"you shall have one manner of law." One law for everyone — and from
that, the rabbis prove the payment reading.

**JACK:** Why does that matter so much?

**MIRIAM:** Because this project just finished the book of Exodus,
and Leviticus is the next book on the desk. Everyone thought of
Leviticus 24 as the first story where a live case walks into the
courtroom Exodus built. It is that. But it turns out it's more:
Exodus's own famous law *can't be finished without it.* The first
book is waiting on the second one the way a sentence waits on its
last word. They didn't guess that — the tracing found it.

**JACK:** So what happens next?

**MIRIAM:** Two things. They keep going through the law sections
that haven't been run yet — the machine found twenty "when"
openings in that code, and nine sections are done, so the list is
known. And there's a boundary they measured: from chapter 23 on,
the "if" word disappears entirely — the law stops speaking in cases
and starts speaking in commandments, "you shall, you shall not." A
different voice. The case-machine only gets to run where the text
speaks in cases, and now they know exactly where that is.

**JACK:** Can I say the thing that's bothering me? If a machine can
grind out the rulebook from the text... doesn't that make the
rabbis unnecessary?

**MIRIAM:** I asked exactly that, and the answer flipped it for me.
The machine can only do this *because* the rabbis wrote everything
down — the arguments, the counts, the usage notes, the
disagreements with names on them. The machine isn't replacing the
teachers. It's the first student in eighteen centuries to take
their notes completely literally — and when it does, the notes
*work*. Sixty percent straight off the page, and every remaining
step, somebody wrote down the reasoning and signed it.

And there's a line the old teachers left that I think about now.
The Talmud tells of a moment when a voice from heaven tried to
settle a legal argument, and a rabbi stood up and quoted the Torah
back: לֹא בַשָּׁמַיִם הִיא — "it is not in heaven." The law was
given; it runs by its own published rules now; even heaven doesn't
get to interrupt. And the story says God *smiled* and said, "My
children have defeated Me, My children have defeated Me."

**JACK:** So the system was built to run on its own.

**MIRIAM:** Built to run on its own, documented by the best teachers
who ever lived, and graded by an answer key that waited eighteen
hundred years for someone to run the test. Forty-four for
forty-four, Jack. And we've only read two books.
