The listening companion to the web page Compiling the Bible (Tutorial_Compiling_The_Bible.html). Same order as the page, part by part. Written 2026, September 4.

## Chapter 1. What This Is, and How to Follow Along

This is the listening companion to a web page called Compiling the Bible. The page and this recording follow the same order, part by part, so you can scroll as you listen. The page carries everything in full: every verse in Hebrew with its English underneath, every passage of the Mishnah and the Talmud quoted whole, diagrams, tables, and real code. This recording deliberately skips the pictures and the long quotations. When the page shows a diagram, I will say so in one sentence and move on. When the page quotes a source in full, I will give you the gist in a breath and let your eyes read the rest if you want it.

The claim the whole page teaches fits in one sentence. The law sections of the Bible are written in a real case structure that a machine can parse. The twenty-four books are the program. The Talmud holds the compile rules. The Mishnah is the answer sheet. And when you write code from the bare verses, and fill each gap with the tradition's own recorded arguments, the code runs, and its output matches the answer sheet, cell for cell.

A quick decoder, because the page assumes no background. Exodus twenty-two six means the book of Exodus, chapter twenty-two, verse six. The Mishnah is the rabbinic law code compiled around the year two hundred, organized into sixty-three tractates by topic. A Mishnah paragraph is a ruling: a case in, a verdict out, and no reasoning shown. The Babylonian Talmud, compiled around the year five hundred, is cited by tractate and page, like Bava Metzia ninety-four b. A Talmud passage is a derivation: it takes a Mishnah ruling and asks, from where do we know this, and walks it back to the verse, keeping the objections and the arguers' names.

A note on Hebrew, since this is for the ear. Hebrew words come in transliteration and every one is glossed immediately in English. When I say ki, that means when. When I say im, that means if. You will never be left holding a foreign word without its meaning.

## Chapter 2. The Claim in One Picture

Part one of the page is a single diagram. It shows four boxes. On the left, the written Bible, all twenty-four books: the program. In the middle, the machine, which parses the verses' own keywords and emits verdict tables. Feeding into the machine from below, the Talmud: the recorded arguments that serve as compile moves. And on the right, the Mishnah: the answer sheet the machine's output is graded against.

Three shelves of books, three roles. The written Bible states the mechanisms, and it states them in a detectable case syntax. The Mishnah states the resulting verdict tables, with no derivations shown. The Talmud is the bridge between them: it records, argument by argument, with names attached, how you get from the verses to the tables. Our job is to make that same trip by machine. Write code from the verses alone. Wherever the bare ink leaves a gap, fill it with the Talmud's recorded argument, never with our own invention. If the claim is true, the finished code reproduces the Mishnah's table. On this page, you watch it happen.

## Chapter 3. Law and Narrative, the Two Halves of a Running System

Part two of the page shows two boxes side by side: law on the left, narrative on the right. Here is what they mean.

The purpose of law is the obvious one. It is the program. The law sections are written in a case grammar carried by three little words. Ki, meaning when, opens a case. Im, meaning if, opens a branch inside it. And o, meaning or, joins alternatives. A scanner that knows nothing but those three tokens recovers the paragraph structure of the law code. And as you will hear in chapter five, the structure it recovers is the very structure the Talmud names.

The purpose of narrative is the half people miss. Code alone is inert. It needs a world to run in: entities that hold state, events that trigger the cases, a clock. The narrative supplies exactly that. It is the population of the simulation, and the record of what actually happened. And the later books do something stronger. They log executions of the earlier code, and they cite the code while doing so. In chapter eight you will hear the book of Chronicles run a law from the book of Numbers at national scale, and stamp its own deviation with the words, not as it is written. A run log, referring back to its source code by name.

One discipline binds the whole machine. When it runs law, its verdicts never invent events. They write a ledger: who owes what, who holds what status, which clocks are running. Obligations are computed. Acts come only from the text.

## Chapter 4. The Method: Five Motions, in Order

Part three of the page is the method box. Five motions, always in this order.

Motion one. Compile from the ink alone. Write the function from the bare verses: the when and if structure, the roles, the events, the outcomes. Anything the ink does not state, a distance, an amount, a threshold, becomes an input parameter, never a constant. No Mishnah and no Talmud in the source code.

Motion two. Collect the answer sheet as test data. Quote the Mishnah's decided cases, input state and expected verdict, into a test file. They are data fed at run time, never code.

Motion three. Run, and print every miss.

Motion four. For each miss, open the Talmud. A miss is where deep logic hides. Find the tradition's recorded argument for that exact gap, and replay it as a labeled compile step, with the source cited on the changed line. Never wholesale, never invented. One gap, one recorded move.

Motion five. When the run reproduces the answer sheet, the function ships, and every cell of its output carries its provenance: ink, or a named recorded argument, or a routed ruling from the answer sheet.

## Chapter 5. The Four Guardians, Part One: the Code and Its Gaps

Now the first worked example, part four of the page, and the flagship of the whole method. One page of Exodus holds the Bible's custody law: what happens when you leave your property in someone else's hands and something goes wrong. Exodus twenty-two, verses six through fourteen. On the page, all nine verses appear in Hebrew and English. Here is what they say.

Verse six opens with ki, when. When a man gives his fellow money or vessels to keep, and it is stolen. If, im, the thief is found, the thief pays double. Verse seven: if the thief is not found, the keeper comes before the judges and swears that he has not laid his hand on his fellow's property, and he goes free. Verse eight adds the fraud clause: a keeper caught swearing falsely pays double himself.

Verse nine opens a second case with the same word, ki, when. When a man gives his fellow a donkey or an ox or a lamb, any animal, to keep, and it dies, or is injured, or is captured, with no witness. Verse ten: an oath of the Lord shall be between the two of them, the same oath formula as before, and the keeper does not pay. But verse eleven: if it is stolen from him, he pays. Verse twelve: if it is torn by a predator, he brings the carcass as evidence, and does not pay.

Now stop and hear the strange thing. Both paragraphs describe someone given property to keep. Same words. And on the identical event, theft, the first keeper swears and goes free, while the second keeper pays. The code never says why. Hold that.

Verse thirteen opens the third case. And when a man borrows from his fellow, and it is injured or dies, its owner not with it, he shall surely pay. This paragraph names its own role: the verb borrows is right there in the ink. The borrower pays even for the accidents that freed the keepers. Verse fourteen closes with two branches: if the owner was with it, no payment. And then four dangling words: if it was hired, it came in its hire. The paragraph ends and explains nothing about them. That dangling clause is a hook, and the answer sheet is about to hang a whole fourth role on it.

The page here shows a diagram of the three paragraphs as three boxes, recovered by a keyword scan that knows nothing but the tokens ki and im. Under it, a yellow box lists the three gaps that honest compilation hits. Gap A, a hidden parameter: something distinguishes the two keepers, and it is not written. Gap B, a missing column: the event of simple loss, the keeper just loses the thing, appears nowhere in the nine verses. Gap C, an unnamed role: the answer sheet is about to list four guardians, and the code has three paragraphs.

Then the page quotes the answer sheet in full: Mishnah Shevuot eight one. It says: there are four keepers. The unpaid keeper, the borrower, the paid keeper, and the hirer. The unpaid keeper swears over everything. The borrower pays everything. The paid keeper and the hirer swear over the injured, the captured, and the dead, and pay for the loss and the theft. Notice everything the table has that the verses do not: the words paid and unpaid, the event loss, and the fourth role, the hirer. Twelve cells of verdict, four roles by three event classes, and no derivation shown. That is what an answer sheet is.

## Chapter 6. The Four Guardians, Part Two: the Talmud's Moves and the Graded Run

Now the compile moves, and here is the wonder of it: every argument we need is already written down, with the objections and the names. The page quotes each passage in Aramaic with English. I will give you each move in a breath.

Move one. Bava Metzia ninety-four b names the structure. The first passage is stated of the unpaid keeper, the second of the paid keeper, the third of the borrower. Three passages: exactly the three when-blocks the keyword scan found. The teacher and the scanner found the same structure, the scanner mechanically, the teacher some eighteen centuries earlier.

Move two. The Talmud demands proof of its own labels. Granted the third is the borrower, it says, that one is explicit in the ink. But the first as unpaid and the second as paid? I could reverse them. In Aramaic, eipukh ana, I could flip it. This is the discipline that makes the tradition usable as compile rules: it will not assert a label without an argument.

Move three, the diff. Feed both paragraphs the same input, theft, and compare outputs. Paragraph one exempts with an oath. Paragraph two pays. Stricter liability tracks compensation, so paragraph two's keeper is the one being paid. The objection is raised on the spot: but paragraph one has the double payment, is that not the stringency? And answered: double fires only on a false oath, paying principal unconditionally is stricter. A hidden configuration parameter, recovered by diffing the branches, on the record. And the principle is sealed at its extreme: the borrower, all the benefit is his, so he carries the severest schedule, paying even for accidents, which is exactly what the ink of verse thirteen says.

Move four, the missing column. Theft is written. Loss is not. The scholars of the land of Israel generate it with a kal va-chomer, an argument from the lighter to the weightier: if theft, which borders on circumstances beyond control, pays, then loss, which borders on negligence, all the more so. The missing cell comes from a recorded inference rule, not from guesswork.

Move five, the fourth role. The hirer is routed onto an existing branch, and the routing is a recorded dispute with names: Rabbi Meir says the hirer pays like the unpaid keeper, Rabbi Yehuda says like the paid keeper, and the law follows Rabbi Yehuda. The four-fold division itself, nobody disputes. This is how a maintained codebase absorbs an input class the original code never named: file it under an existing branch, and log the disagreement.

The page then shows a short excerpt of the real runnable program, and under it the graded table, with a color on every cell telling you where that verdict came from. Here is the result. Twelve of twelve cells match Mishnah Shevuot eight one. Six cells straight from ink. Three by recorded arguments replayed step for step. Three routed exactly as the answer sheet routes them. Nothing invented, and provenance on every cell. That is the whole method, executed once, end to end.

## Chapter 7. The Slave's Clock and the Jubilee

The second worked example, part five of the page, teaches two new shapes: a timer, and an interrupt that crosses between books. The law is the very first case of the Exodus law code, chapter twenty-one, verses two through six. The page quotes all five verses. Here is the flow.

When you acquire a Hebrew slave, six years he shall serve, and in the seventh he goes out free, for nothing. A case opener carrying a clock. The slave here is an indentured servant, typically a debtor working off what he cannot repay, and the same chapter gives him rights his master cannot touch. Then the branches. If he came alone, he leaves alone. If married, his wife leaves with him. If the master gave him a wife, she and her children stay, and he leaves alone. And then: if the slave plainly says, I love my master, my wife, and my children, I will not go out free, then his master brings him to the doorpost and pierces his ear with an awl, and he shall serve him le-olam. Forever.

So the six-year timer is voided by the slave's own declaration, and the code says forever. Does forever mean forever? Exodus, read alone, gives no way out. Now turn four books over, to Leviticus twenty-five, verse ten, the Jubilee. Sanctify the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty, deror, in the land to all its inhabitants. A yovel, a jubilee, it shall be for you. And you shall return every man to his holding, and every man to his family. A global event on a fifty-year clock. Nothing in it mentions the pierced slave of Exodus. Or does it?

The Talmud, in tractate Kiddushin page fifteen, reads the two verses against each other and shows they were written for each other. First, by elimination: every other release is already covered by some verse, so the return clause of Leviticus must be covering the one case nothing else covers, the man who said forever. The jubilee takes him out. And then the beautiful part, which the page quotes in full. The Talmud says both verses had to be written. Had the Torah written only forever, I would say, forever literally. So it wrote, you shall return. And had it written only, you shall return, I would think the release covers only someone still inside his six years. So it teaches: forever means, unto the era of the jubilee. Two clauses in two books, each guarding against the misreading of the other. The word forever is a scoped constant, and its scope is set in a different book.

The answer sheet states the merged result as a plain table, Mishnah Kiddushin one two, quoted in full on the page. A Hebrew slave acquires himself by the years, by the jubilee, and by deduction of money. The pierced one acquires himself by the jubilee and by the death of the master. Read that release list like an engineer. The years: Exodus's own timer. Deduction of money: a computed early exit. And the jubilee: an event declared in a different book entirely. The answer sheet's table is a merge of two books' code, and the Talmud passage is the merge documentation.

The page closes this part with a diagram: Exodus's timeline with the six-year clock, the forever branch running off its end, and a red interrupt arrow crossing in from a Leviticus box and cutting it. In the running simulator this is literally implemented as timer cancellation. The books behave like modules of one program.

## Chapter 8. The Second Passover: Code, Data, and the Run Log

The third worked example, part six of the page, shows where narrative earns its place, and gives the cleanest picture of what is code and what is data. Numbers chapter nine records a law being born from a case. The page quotes six verses. Here is the story they tell.

Certain men were ritually impure from contact with the dead, and so barred from the Passover offering, through no fault of their own. They file their complaint in their own words: why should we be diminished? Moses answers: stand, and I will hear what the Lord commands concerning you. Hold the case, query the Author, await new code. The written Torah records that same hold-and-ask protocol four times. And the new code arrives, in the same case syntax as Exodus: any man, ki, when, he is impure by a corpse, o, or, on a distant journey, shall perform the Passover in the second month, on the fourteenth day. And the else branch is closed explicitly: one who is pure, and present, and simply refrains, is cut off.

Two details in the arriving code matter. First, the word distant carries no number. Second, on the Hebrew of that verse, there is a small dot above the last letter of the word rechokah, distant. That dot is in the scroll itself, one of fifteen dotted spots in the whole Bible. The page shows it in the ink.

Now the gap. How far is distant? Under this method that is not a defect. It is a parameter. The threshold is data, and the tradition preserves the data with two recorded settings, names attached. Mishnah Pesachim nine two, quoted in full on the page: what is a distant journey? From the town of Modiim and beyond, about fifteen miles, the words of Rabbi Akiva. Rabbi Eliezer says: from the threshold of the Temple courtyard and beyond, one step outside. And Rabbi Yosei adds: therefore there is a dot over the letter heh, to say, not because he is truly distant. The dot you just saw in the ink, read by the tradition as the scroll's own footnote qualifying the parameter. Code in ink. Quantities as data. Even the annotation layer is real.

Now the payoff. Roughly seven centuries after Numbers, the book of Chronicles records King Hezekiah restarting the Passover after a period of national neglect. Second Chronicles chapter thirty, quoted on the page. The king and the assembly resolve to perform the Passover in the second month, because the priests had not sanctified themselves in sufficient number and the people had not gathered. That is the deferral branch of Numbers nine, invoked at national scale, with the disqualifying conditions logged as reasons. And then the astonishing verse. A majority of the northern tribes had not purified themselves, yet they ate the Passover, and the log stamps it: be-lo kha-katuv. Not as it is written. The history log measures its own run against the written code and records the deviation. Hezekiah prays, and the Lord heals the people: the exception escalated past the law to the Author, and answered, and logged as such.

And the teaching layer audits this very run. The Talmud, in Sanhedrin twelve b, reviews Hezekiah's Passover as an edge case, opening its review by quoting the run log's own line, and cross-examining the run against yet another book's code: the calendar rule of Exodus twelve two, this month is for you the head of months. Code, run log, and audit, all inside the one library. The page closes the part with a three-box diagram: the code, the run log, the audit, each citing the layer before it.

The page then adds three more cases, and they show the second pass this whole method is heading for: the narrative supplying input to functions already compiled from the law. Each one arrives as speech, someone stating the facts to a judge or to the other party, and the speech is the field list. Each one lands on a function you have already heard built on this page. And each one was checked, phrase by phrase, against the whole local shelf of the Talmud, the Mishnah, the Tosefta, and the midrash, the rabbinic commentary collections, to ask whether the tradition ever treated it as a case. It never did. Where it quotes these verses at all, it quotes them for something else.

The first is the borrowed axe head, Second Kings six, verse five. A disciple of Elisha is felling a beam and the iron head flies off into the Jordan, and he cries out: alas, my master, ve-hu sha'ul, and it was borrowed. That cry is the case. The verb is the same verb that opens the borrower's paragraph in Exodus twenty-two thirteen. The event is loss. The owner is nowhere in the scene. Three fields, and the borrower's row of the guardians' table returns its verdict: pay, for everything. The narrator never needs to say so. The cry says it: the loss is a catastrophe precisely because a borrower pays. Two verses later Elisha floats the iron and the disciple takes it back, and the ledger line closes by return of the object, not by payment. The Talmud quotes this verse exactly once, in Sotah thirteen a, to prove that iron can float. The borrower's row it fits went unused.

The second is a parable a prophet tells to King Ahab, First Kings twenty, verses thirty-nine and forty. A soldier says: a man brought me a captive and said, shemor, guard this man; if he goes missing, your life for his life, or you weigh out a talent of silver. And as your servant was busy here and there, he was gone. The king answers: so is your judgment, you yourself have decided it. Hear the fields. A deposit, with the very verb of Exodus twenty-two's keeper paragraphs. A penalty stipulated at delivery, which the bare ink has no clause for, and which the answer sheet supplies: Mishnah Bava Metzia seven ten records that keepers set their own terms. And negligence, the keeper who left his post, the exact shape the Talmud tests on Bava Metzia ninety-three b with a shepherd who left his flock and went to town. Function and king agree: liable. The prophet built the case so the king would convict himself, since the captive Ahab had let go was Ben-Hadad. The Babylonian Talmud never quotes the parable. The Jerusalem Talmud quotes it once, for Ahab's sentence, not for the deposit.

The third is Joseph's cup, Genesis forty-four. The cup is planted in Benjamin's sack, and the brothers set their own penalty before the search: whoever is found with it dies, and the rest of us become slaves. The Egyptian steward corrects them on the spot: the one found with it becomes my slave, and you go clear. Then the cup is found in his hand, Judah offers all of them as slaves, and Joseph refuses: the man in whose hand the cup was found, he shall be my slave, and you go up in peace. Now lay Exodus twenty-two, verses two and three, beside it. If the theft is found in his hand, he pays double; if he has nothing, he is sold for his theft. Theft is never capital, and the code addresses the thief alone. The brothers' proposal is out of range on both counts. The steward's ruling, and Joseph's, land inside the code's own contour: one man, servitude, nobody else touched. Whatever Joseph knew about how the cup got there, the case as posed runs through the function and returns the steward's verdict. The Talmud touches this chapter once, Shabbat one hundred five a, reading Judah's words as an acrostic. Never as theft law.

One honest counter-example belongs beside these. Jacob's speech to Laban in Genesis thirty-one, I bore the loss, stolen by day or stolen by night, has the same shape for the paid keeper's row, and there the tradition did carry it to the law page: Bava Metzia ninety-three b uses Jacob's line about heat by day and frost by night to set the keeper's duty of care. So the teacher read narrative as case input at least once, in the very sugya this page compiled. The three you just heard are the ones left on the shelf. And the extraction of the fields from the prose was done by hand, as it was for Chronicles. That is the open work, and the page says so.

## Chapter 9. A Gallery of Code Shapes

Part seven of the page is a gallery: six cards, each a code shape a programmer recognizes on sight, each with its source quoted in full. I will give you each card in a breath or two.

Card one, the state machine: the goring ox, Exodus twenty-one. An ox carries a state variable with two values. Tam, innocuous: it pays half damages, only out of its own body. Muad, forewarned: full damages. The transition threshold is encoded in the verse's own time words, mi-temol shilshom, from yesterday and the day before, and the Talmud records two named tokenizations of that phrase, by Abaye and by Rava, both landing on three gorings as the flip. The Mishnah even records the reverse transition: the ox returns to innocuous when it holds back three days, or, in Rabbi Meir's version, when children can handle it and it does not gore. A state machine with a forward transition, a threshold, and a decay transition.

Card two, the class hierarchy: Mishnah Bava Kamma one one. Four primary categories of damage, and the Hebrew word for categories is avot, literally fathers: the ox, the pit, the grazer, and the fire. The Mishnah then compares their traits pairwise and states their common interface: their way is to damage, their safekeeping is on you, and the damager pays from the best of the land. Those four fathers are four consecutive when-cases in Exodus, in order. And fathers implies offspring: the Talmud's opening pages sort which derivative damages inherit which parent's rules. A class hierarchy with inheritance, argued in the tradition's own vocabulary.

Card three, the bounded loop: the skin examination of Leviticus thirteen. Examine the mark. If inconclusive, quarantine seven days. Re-examine. At most one more pass, and the loop's iteration counter is written in the ink: the word shenit, a second time, appears twice. Bounded at two passes, with a re-entry guard right after: if the mark spreads after release, back to impure.

Card four, the access list: Mishnah Yoma seven five. The breastplate oracle of Exodus twenty-eight answers only when the High Priest wears the full eight garments, and only for three privileged callers: the king, the court, and one whom the community needs. An interface with an authorization layer.

Card five, the language manual. The tradition documents the keywords themselves. Reish Lakish, in tractate Gittin: the word ki serves four language senses, if, perhaps, rather, because. And the Mekhilta, the earliest law commentary on Exodus, runs a corpus-wide census of the branch keyword: every im, every if, in the Torah is optional, except three, and it lists the three mandatory ones. A keyword census with its exceptions enumerated. That is what a language manual is.

Card six, effects. Look back at the outputs of every law on this page. He shall pay double: a debt entry. He goes out free: a status change. That soul shall be cut off: an entry on Heaven's docket, no earthly court involved. You shall return every man to his holding: a mass transfer on a timer. The verdicts' verbs are the simulation's effect vocabulary, discovered in the ink, never designed. Every verdict writes the ledger, and never invents an event.

## Chapter 10. The Interconnected Program: an Eye for an Eye

Part eight of the page is the deepest case: a famous Exodus law whose actual meaning is compiled through a clause in Leviticus. The Exodus text everyone has heard: an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth, a hand for a hand. Read alone, it sounds like mirror-image retaliation. The tradition has always ruled it as monetary compensation, the value of the eye, assessed and paid. Is that a dodge? Watch where the ruling actually comes from.

The derivation lives in Leviticus twenty-four, which the page quotes in full, six verses. That passage restates the injury laws, and does something structural: it sets human injury right next to animal injury, twice, using the same striking verb, and the animal clause explicitly says, shall pay for it. And it closes with an equality clause: mishpat echad, one law shall there be for you.

The Talmud, in Bava Kamma eighty-three b, reads the structure as an executable instruction. You might think: he blinded his eye, we blind his eye. The verse teaches: one who strikes a man, and one who strikes a beast. Just as the striker of a beast is for payment, so the striker of a man is for payment. The shared verb carries the payment semantics across. And on the next page, Rabbi Shimon ben Yochai seals it with the equality clause. Suppose a blind man blinded someone. An amputee severed a hand. How do I fulfill an eye for an eye literally in such a case? And the Torah said: one law shall there be for you, a law equal for all of you. Literal retaliation cannot execute uniformly on every input. Only monetary valuation passes the equality constraint declared in Leviticus. In programming terms: the true meaning of the Exodus verse is not in the Exodus verse. It is a measured compile dependency on another book.

The page then draws the map: book boxes for Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Chronicles, with an arrow for every edge this tutorial itself established. Forever, scoped by the jubilee. Eye for eye, compiled as payment through one law. The run log of Chronicles citing the code of Numbers. And the audit of Sanhedrin grading that run against the calendar rule of Exodus. Every edge on the map was quoted and argued on the page.

Under the map the page lists three more edges it did not draw, the three narrative cases from chapter eight: Second Kings six to the borrower's row, First Kings twenty to the keepers' rows with the Mishnah's stipulation rule alongside, and Genesis forty-four to the theft law of Exodus twenty-two. Genesis sits before Exodus in the scroll, and the steward's ruling still lands inside Exodus's range. That is the kind of edge the walk from both directions is meant to find.

How are these edges found? From both directions. Walking the scroll, you compile each span in order and hit imports. Walking the Talmud, each case discussion names its verse list across all the books, a test's dependency closure, and a verse from a book you have not compiled yet is an arrow telling you the program is bigger than the page you are on. Run this over the whole library, and the twenty-four books stop looking like an anthology and start looking like what the tradition always called them: one Torah. One interconnected program, with its histories as the run log.

## Chapter 11. What Is Honestly Claimed, and What Comes Next

Part nine of the page is the honesty box, and it matters. The keyword parse, the token extraction, and the grading runs are fully mechanical, and the graded scores, including the guardians' twelve of twelve, are real program output, reproducible from the files. The compile moves are hand-encoded replays of the Talmud's recorded arguments. The machine executes the tradition's logic. It does not discover it. That is exactly the claim under test: not that a computer invents the law, but that the written books carry a real program, and the tradition's recorded reasoning is that program's engineering documentation, precise enough to execute. Every quotation on the page was pulled from the source texts at the letter. The disagreements stay disagreements, both arms labeled, in the code as in the books.

Where this goes. Walk the scroll span by span. Compile every law into a function that carries effects. Let the Mishnah's thousands of decided cases grade each one, and let every miss send you to the Talmud's recorded argument for that gap. Each verdict writes a ledger over the world the narrative supplies: debts, statuses, running clocks. And then the question this whole workshop is built to ask. Run that ledger across the entire Hebrew Bible's run log, and see whether the prophets' indictments match the entries the law had already computed as open. Nobody has ever computed that. The books are waiting, and the method is on the page in front of you. End of book.
