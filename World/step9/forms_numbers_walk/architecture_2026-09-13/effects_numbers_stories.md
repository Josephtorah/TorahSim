## Story 9 — the command and its receipt: a debt the order opens, the doing closes, and a count that becomes a row

**The story, in plain words.** Exodus gives the order for a census —
"when you take the sum" — and does not take it. Numbers takes it, on
its first page. The same happens with the camp's layout, the count of
the Levites, the count of the firstborn, the redemption money: an
earlier book orders, this book does. The text keeps its own receipts
for this: the verse that reports the doing very often ends "as the
LORD commanded Moses." The program reads an order as a debt and the
receipt as its close. The count itself is not a debt but a number —
six hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty — and the
program keeps it the way the text does, as a table, tribe by tribe.
And some of the orders in this book are not done in this book at all:
the cities for the Levites, the six cities of refuge. Their doing is
in Joshua. The debts stand open at the Torah's end, on purpose, each
with the verse that will close it named on the entry.

**What changed, one line each.**
- At the order: a debt on the people's account — "commanded" — whose
  value is the thing ordered.
- At the doing: the debt closed, with the receipt verse named as the
  closer; the count written as a status carrying the number, and as
  rows in the population table.
- At an order whose doing lies in a later book: the debt left open,
  the closing verse named.

**The verses.** The order, three books before. Listen for "when."
Exodus 30:12:

> "When you take the sum of the children of Israel by their numbered
> ones, each shall give a ransom for his soul to the LORD when they
> are numbered, so that there is no plague among them when they are
> numbered."

The doing. Numbers 1:2-3:

> "Take the sum of all the congregation of the children of Israel by
> their families, by their fathers' houses, by the number of names,
> every male by their skulls. From twenty years old and upward,
> everyone who goes out to the army in Israel — you shall number them
> by their hosts, you and Aaron."

And the receipt, sixteen verses on. Numbers 1:19:

> "As the LORD commanded Moses, so he numbered them in the wilderness
> of Sinai."

The formula is כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוָּה יְהוָה אֶת מֹשֶׁה (ka'asher tzivvah
Adonai et Moshe, "as the LORD commanded Moses"). Now an order whose
doing is a book away. Numbers 35:2:

> "Command the children of Israel that they give to the Levites, from
> the inheritance of their possession, cities to dwell in; and
> pasture-land for the cities around them you shall give to the
> Levites."

And its receipt, in the sixth book. Joshua 21:3:

> "And the children of Israel gave to the Levites from their
> inheritance, at the mouth of the LORD, these cities and their
> pasture-lands."

**What the tradition says.** The Talmud asks the ledger's own
subtraction (Babylonian Talmud Bekhorot 5a). The Levite houses, added
one by one, come to 22,300; the verse that totals them writes 22,000.
Where did the three hundred go? Its answer: they were firstborn
themselves, and a firstborn Levite could stand in for no firstborn of
Israel. The program does not correct the verse. It reads the sum,
reads the total, reports the three hundred as the text's own delta,
and carries the Talmud's answer as a data row beside it.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "Commanded" is a DEBIT
written by a specification on its addressee; its value is the thing
commanded. The verse that narrates the doing closes it, and since the
fourth book a close is a line of its own in the program's log, naming
the day and the closing verse. The text's receipt formula is read by a
gate of its own: fifty-eight seats of the short form and eleven of the
long, "according to all that the LORD commanded," and for each the
gate asks the ledger what it closed. "Counted" is a STATUS carrying the
number the reader took off the verse. And the count is also a ROW — a
line in the population table, tribe by tribe and family by family —
which only a rule consuming a verse may write; a hand may not. At the
book's end the table holds 148 rows, and Israel's account carries
fourteen commands, seven of them closed by a verse and seven open to
Joshua's runs.

**For the record.** Of the first census's five orders — the count, the
camp, the Levites, the firstborn, the redemption — all five are closed
inside the book's first four chapters, each by a receipt verse. Of the
book's last law chapter's two, none is: the receipt for the Levites'
forty-eight cities is Joshua 21:41, and the program's reader counts
Joshua's four lots to exactly forty-eight.

## Story 10 — the dedication and the appointed days: a change written into the past, and a duty keyed to the calendar

**The story, in plain words.** Numbers 7 tells the twelve princes'
offerings for the altar's dedication, one prince a day for twelve
days. But the chapter is dated to the day the tabernacle was finished
— a month before the census that opens the book — so by the time the
program reads it, its clock already stands past all twelve days. The
order "one prince per day" is an order whose twelve doings are already
past. The program writes them anyway, each dated to its own day, with
the clock not moved back. Then Numbers 28 and 29 lay out the
offerings calendar: the daily lamb of Story 8, and on top of it an
additional offering on every Sabbath, every new moon, and every
festival day. The daily lamb's duty came back every day; these come
back on days only a calendar can name — "the next Sabbath," "the next
first of the month," "the next Passover."

**What changed, one line each.**
- At "one prince per day": twelve entries, one on each prince, each
  dated to a day already past when the order is read.
- At "the burnt offering of the Sabbath on its Sabbath": a recurring
  debt on the altar's account, keyed to the calendar's Sabbath; seven
  more for the month and the festivals.
- The daily lamb's own standing debt, open since the altar was
  raised: read by the new rule, not written a second time.

**The verses.** The order and its first doing. Listen for the phrase
said twice. Numbers 7:11-12:

> "And the LORD said to Moses: one prince per day, one prince per day,
> they shall bring near their offering for the dedication of the
> altar. And he who brought near his offering on the first day was
> Nahshon son of Amminadab, of the tribe of Judah."

The doubled phrase is נָשִׂיא אֶחָד לַיּוֹם (nasi echad la-yom, "one
prince per day"). Now the calendar's rule. Listen for "on its
Sabbath" and "beside." Numbers 28:10:

> "The burnt offering of the Sabbath on its Sabbath, beside the
> continual burnt offering and its libation."

And the calendar's close, Numbers 29:39:

> "These you shall offer to the LORD at your appointed times, besides
> your vows and your freewill offerings, for your burnt offerings and
> for your meal offerings and for your libations and for your peace
> offerings."

**What the tradition says.** The Sifrei on Numbers (47:1) reads the
order of the twelve days off the text: Moses did not know whether the
princes should bring by their birth order, Reuben first, or by the
order of the march, Judah first, until he was told — by the march.
The program computes the same thing: the twelve days' princes are the
camp's order of chapter 2, not the birth order. And the Talmud (Moed
Katan 9a) reads the twelve days as one continuous run with a Sabbath
inside it. For the additional offerings the tradition has a name of
its own, musaf, "the addition," and a rule about it: the daily and the
additional offerings do not hold each other up (Mishnah Menachot 4:4)
— if one is missing, the other is still brought.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** A RETRO-WRITE is a
ledger write dated behind the clock. The rule that consumes "one
prince per day" asks for the anointing day — the date at 7:1, which
the program had already placed a month before the census — and writes
twelve "dedication offered" statuses on the twelve princes, each due
that day plus its count, every due already past. The program's log
keeps a class for such lines, and the tape carries exactly twelve;
the check is that each due equals the day the text itself stamps on
verses 7:12 to 7:78. "The additional offering owed" is a DEBIT with a
PERIOD, like the daily lamb's — but its period is a calendar word, not
a number of days: the timer asks the calendar for the next Sabbath,
the next new moon, the next first day of Passover, and re-arms itself
on each fire. Eight such timers stand on the altar's account, their
values the tables of bulls, rams, lambs and goats the reader took off
the verses. And the daily lamb's own debt, open on the altar since the
day it was raised, is read by the new rule and not written again: one
act, one writer.

**For the record.** "The burnt offering of the Sabbath on its Sabbath"
stands at one place in the whole Bible. The word "beside," which
stacks the day's offerings on the daily one, stands twelve times
between 28:1 and 29:39, and the program reads the stack off it.

## Story 11 — the docket and the rule installed: the change a case makes to the law itself

**The story, in plain words.** Every change so far was written on an
account — a person, an animal, a house, the land. This one is written
on the law. A man is found gathering wood on the Sabbath. The Sabbath
law is in force and its penalty is death — Exodus said so — but the
text says the *manner* of death had not been declared. So the man is
put in custody and the story stops until the answer comes. The answer
comes as a verse: stoning, by all the congregation, outside the camp.
That verse does two things at once. It sentences this man, and it
installs a rule into a law that already stood, for every case after.
Numbers has four such halts (Leviticus one), and the program was
built to stop at each: a docket that opens, an answer verse that
installs, a deed that closes.

**What changed, one line each.**
- At "they put him in custody": a body entry on the man, open; and a
  declaration owed on the court's docket.
- At the answer verse: a rule installed — a status on an institution,
  its value the law it joins; the docket's entry closed.
- At "and he died": the custody, the death sentence and the stoning
  all closed by the deed.

**The verses.** The halt. Listen for the reason the text gives.
Numbers 15:34:

> "And they put him in custody, because it had not been declared what
> should be done to him."

The words are לֹא פֹרַשׁ (lo forash, "it had not been declared"). The
answer and the deed. Numbers 15:35-36:

> "And the LORD said to Moses: the man shall surely be put to death;
> stone him with stones, all the congregation, outside the camp. And
> all the congregation brought him outside the camp and stoned him
> with stones, and he died, as the LORD commanded Moses."

The other halts say their halt in their own words. Numbers 9:8, the
men unclean at Passover: "And Moses said to them: stand, and I will
hear what the LORD commands concerning you." Numbers 27:5, the
daughters of Zelophehad: "And Moses brought their judgment near
before the LORD."

**What the tradition says.** The Sifrei on Numbers (114) reads the
verse exactly as the program does — the penalty already stood, the
mode did not, and the answer is two things:

> "But is it not written (Shemot 31:14) 'He who profanes it shall be
> put to death'? What, then, is the intent of 'For it was not made
> clear'? He did not know with what specific type of death until it
> was told to Him by the Holy One. 'And the L-rd said to Moses: Die,
> shall die the man': this is the judgment for all the generations.
> 'stone him with stones': in this particular instance."

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "In custody" is a BODY
entry — the registry's own row from the prison in Genesis 40, reused
here — written on the man and open. The declaration owed sits on the
court's DOCKET, the program's list of cases no rule decided; the
running world's docket holds four, each with the verse that closed
it. "The rule installed" is a STATUS written on an institution — the
tent of meeting, or the priesthood — whose value is the law it
installs, named in the case's name; the running world holds seven
such rows, and the tradition's own dispute about what the answer is,
a rule for the generations or an edict for this instance, is a
setting with the other arm named. The deed closes the rest. Here is
the man's whole account as the program prints it today, five rows,
the verse texts cut short:

```
  labor_barred                 | block  | written_by=law_sabbath   | verse=Num 15:32-33 | state=written
  put_to_death                 | body   | written_by=law_sabbath   | closed_by=Num 15:36 | state=closed
  stoned                       | body   | written_by=law_sabbath   | closed_by=Num 15:36 | state=closed
  warned_specifying_the_labor  | status | written_by=law_mekoshesh | verse=Num 15:32-33 | state=written
  in_custody                   | body   | written_by=law_tent      | closed_by=Num 15:36 | state=closed
```

What to notice in the third column: the block and the death sentence
were written by the Sabbath rule installed in Exodus — the first time
that rule ever fired on the tape — and the custody by the tent's own
rule. A law from one book, a case from another, one account.

**For the record.** The phrase "a statute of judgment" — the text's
own name for a rule for the generations — stands at exactly two
places in the Torah: Numbers 27:11, the daughters' answer, and
Numbers 35:29, the refuge cities. The program found both.

## Story 12 — the manslayer's term: a change that ends at a death nobody can date

**The story, in plain words.** A man kills without meaning to. The
avenger of blood may pursue him, so the law gives him six cities to
run to, and the court delivers him from the avenger's hand and sends
him back to his city — to live there "until the death of the high
priest." Then he goes home. Compare the servant of Story 1: his term
had a length, six years, and the program could set a clock. The
manslayer's term has an end but no length. Nobody knows when the high
priest will die. So the program cannot set a timer. It writes an open
entry that carries the name of the priest in office, and closes it
when the text records that priest's death — and only that priest's.

**What changed, one line each.**
- At the court's deliverance: "dwells in refuge" on the manslayer, a
  body entry, open, its value the name of the high priest in office.
- No timer set — the text gives no day.
- At "the high priest died": the entry closed, by the name it carried;
  "returns to the land of his possession" written on him.

**The verses.** The cities. Listen for "unintentionally." Numbers
35:11:

> "And you shall appoint for yourselves cities, cities of refuge they
> shall be for you; and a manslayer who strikes a soul unintentionally
> shall flee there."

The term. Listen for the end that is named and the day that is not.
Numbers 35:25:

> "And the congregation shall deliver the manslayer from the hand of
> the avenger of blood, and the congregation shall return him to his
> city of refuge, where he had fled; and he shall dwell in it until
> the death of the high priest, who was anointed with the holy oil."

Those last words are עַד מוֹת הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדֹל (ad mot ha-kohen
ha-gadol, "until the death of the high priest"). And the release,
Numbers 35:28:

> "For in his city of refuge he shall dwell until the death of the
> high priest; and after the death of the high priest the manslayer
> shall return to the land of his possession."

**What the tradition says.** The Mishnah's table for this law (Mishnah
Makkot 2:6) is a table of which death ends which term. Three kinds of
high priest end it — the one anointed with oil, the one who wore the
many garments, the one who served and was relieved. If the high priest
died after the verdict, the man goes home; if he died before the
verdict was given and another was appointed, the man goes home at the
second priest's death. And the mothers of the high priests, it
records, used to feed and clothe the exiles, so that they would not
pray for their sons to die. The Talmud (Babylonian Talmud Makkot 11a)
counts the words: "the death of the high priest" is written three
times in these verses, one for each of the three.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "Dwells in refuge" is a
BODY entry — the same kind as custody in Story 11 — written open, with
a VALUE: the name of the office-holder, which the refuge rule gets by
a call into the law of chapter 20, where Eleazar took the office at
Aaron's death. "The high priest died" is an EVENT, and when it arrives
the rule closes every open "dwells in refuge" whose value is that
priest's name — a close BY VALUE — and writes "returns to the land of
his possession," a STATUS, on each. Nothing on the tape sends anyone
to a city in the Torah's own story, so the running world holds no
such entry; the program's test world runs the tradition's cases, and
there a man sentenced under Eleazar goes home at Eleazar's death while
a man sentenced under Phinehas stays through it and goes home at
Phinehas's — the Mishnah's row, and the reason the entry is keyed by a
name rather than by the office. Eleazar's death is Joshua 24:33,
outside the Torah: the closer by design, like the debts of Story 9.

**For the record.** "He has no blood" — the words that free the
avenger who kills the manslayer outside his city (35:27) — is the
burglar's clause from Exodus 22:1, in the singular here and the plural
there; the program reuses the burglar's own effect rather than writing
a second. One effect, two laws, as "goes free" was one effect with
three triggers in Story 1.

## Story 13 — the vow: a debt toward Heaven, and the day her father hears it

**The story, in plain words.** A man vows, and is bound by his own
word: "he shall not profane his word." Nobody collects this debt but
Heaven — it is Story 4's kind of entry, opened by the vower himself. A
daughter in her father's house, or a wife, is bound the same way, with
one difference: her father or her husband may confirm the vow or annul
it, and only on the day he hears of it. If he is silent through that
day, the vow stands. If he restrains her that day, the vow falls "and
the LORD will forgive her." And if he annuls it after having let it
stand, the annulment is void, she is clear — and "he shall bear her
iniquity." The chapter has no numbers at all except its clocks: "on
the day he hears," "from day to day," "after he has heard."

**What changed, one line each.**
- At the vow: a debt on the vower toward Heaven, its value the vow.
- At the hearing: a clock to the day's end. Silence through it
  confirms — a status on the vow. Restraint before it cancels the
  clock, closes the debt, and writes Heaven's "annulled": the LORD
  forgives her.
- At an annulment after the confirmation: nothing on her; "her
  iniquity borne," Heaven's entry, on the husband.

**The verses.** The man's vow. Listen for "profane" and "all."
Numbers 30:3:

> "A man, when he vows a vow to the LORD, or swears an oath to bind a
> bond upon his soul, shall not profane his word; according to all
> that goes out of his mouth he shall do."

The bond is לֹא יַחֵל דְּבָרוֹ (lo yachel devaro, "he shall not profane
his word"). The daughter, and the day. Listen for "on the day he
hears" and the last clause. Numbers 30:6:

> "But if her father restrains her on the day he hears, none of her
> vows or her bonds that she bound upon her soul shall stand; and the
> LORD will forgive her, because her father restrained her."

The day is בְּיוֹם שָׁמְעוֹ (be-yom shom'o, "on the day he hears"). And
the late annulment, Numbers 30:16:

> "But if he annuls them after he has heard, then he shall bear her
> iniquity."

**What the tradition says.** The Sifrei on Numbers (156:1) reads the
clock two ways off the chapter's own words and keeps both. "From day
to day" — twenty-four hours? No: "he has confirmed them, for he was
silent on the day of his hearing" — until nightfall. And Rabbi Shimon
ben Yochai: twenty-four hours, for "the Torah said from day to day."
On the late annulment the Sifrei (156:2) reads "after he has heard" as
after he has confirmed, and "he shall bear her iniquity" as: he enters
in her place for the sin.

**Now the program's terms, in one paragraph.** "The vow bound" is a
DEBIT toward HEAVEN — a debt with no earthly collector, like the cut
off of Story 4, but opened by the vower's own mouth; its value is the
vow. The hearing day is a TIMER, the engine's own, due at the end of
the day of hearing; its fire writes "the vow confirmed," a STATUS.
Restraint before the fire cancels the timer, closes the debt by
value, and writes "the vow annulled" — HEAVEN's entry, whose verse
ends with the same closing formula as the sin offering's, "and the
LORD will forgive her." Annulment after the fire writes nothing on
her and "her iniquity borne," HEAVEN's entry, on the husband. A father
over a married woman, or a husband over a widow, writes nothing at
all: the wrong authority. The two settings of the clock, nightfall and
twenty-four hours, are a calendar row with two arms, the first
running and the second kept; and two questions the Talmud leaves
standing are carried as rows marked unresolved. The program's test
ran the tradition's cases through this machine: eight timers set,
five fired, three cancelled, every number predicted by script before
the run and matched on the first.

**For the record.** "And the LORD will forgive her" stands at three
places in the whole Bible, all three in this chapter (30:6, 30:9,
30:13). "On the day of his hearing" stands four times in the chapter,
"from day to day" once, "after his hearing" once — the chapter's only
numbers are its clocks, and the program found no other.
